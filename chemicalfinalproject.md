Introduction
The progressive intensification of urbanization, fossil fuel combustion, and industrial expansion has precipitated a severe degradation of global atmospheric quality. At the absolute core of this environmental crisis is airborne Particulate Matter (PM), functioning as a primary mechanism for both ecosystem instability and a massive global burden of respiratory and cardiovascular disease.
1.1. Physical Characteristics and Classifications of PM Particulate Matter, colloquially categorized as particle pollution, is an operationally defined scientific term for a highly complex, heterogeneous mixture of solid particles and liquid droplets suspended within the atmospheric column (US EPA, 2023). The physical dimensions of these suspended entities vary exponentially. Certain macroscopic elements, such as wind-blown dirt, combustion soot, and construction dust, possess sufficient mass and circumference to be visually detected by the naked eye. Conversely, the vast majority of suspended particles are entirely microscopic, requiring advanced electron microscopy for structural detection.
To standardize environmental regulation and toxicological risk assessment, atmospheric particles are universally classified by their aerodynamic diameters:
•	PM₁₀: Coarse inhalable particles characterized by aerodynamic diameters generally equal to or less than 10 micrometers (µm).
•	PM₂.₅: Fine inhalable particles characterized by aerodynamic diameters generally equal to or less than 2.5 µm.

Primary and Secondary Emission Origins These pollutants enter our local environments through two very different physical pathways. Primary particles are ejected directly from a specific geographic origin into the air. Daily industrial activities, traffic across unpaved roads, large-scale construction zones, agricultural clearing, and direct exhaust from open fires or industrial smokestacks all contribute heavily to direct primary particulate loading.
However, the overwhelming majority of fine particulate loads actually function as secondary particles. These microscopic fragments do not emerge physically complete from a local smokestack. Instead, they form through complex photochemical reactions in the open atmosphere. Gaseous precursors, specifically sulphur dioxide (SO₂) and nitrogen oxides (NOₓ) emitted heavily by municipal power plants and internal combustion vehicle engines, react with sunlight and atmospheric moisture to precipitate outward into solid sulphates and liquid chemical droplets.
Health and Environmental Pathogenesis The extraordinarily small size of these particles directly determines their lethality. Extensive epidemiological reports from the World Health Organization currently attribute millions of premature global deaths strictly to conditions induced by systemic PM exposure (WHO, 2023). While the human body naturally traps large environmental dust in the upper airway, these microscopic pollutants easily evade our innate respiratory defences. Inhaled PM₁₀ fragments travel deep into the lower lungs, physically irritating pulmonary tissue and significantly worsening established respiratory conditions like chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.
Fine particles (PM₂.₅) present a much more severe systemic physiological threat. Their minute size allows them to effortlessly cross the alveolar barrier within the lungs and slip directly into the human circulatory system (US EPA, 2023). Once circulating actively in the bloodstream, these fragments trigger widespread vascular inflammation and intense cellular stress throughout the body. This internal degradation acts as a direct catalyst for critical cardiovascular events, drastically inflating the clinical risk of ischemic heart disease, strokes, and fatal cardiac arrhythmias. Beyond this devastating physiological cost, highly concentrated fine particles are absolutely responsible for severe environmental haze. This persistent visual pollution deeply diminishes functional visibility across urban commercial centres and profoundly degrades the natural condition of treasured national parks and protected wilderness preserves.

The severity of particulate matter loading is intensely localized, and current empirical data places the Indian subcontinent at the absolute epicentre of the crisis. According to the 2024 IQAir World Air Quality Report, India recorded an annual average PM₂.₅ concentration of 50.6 µg/m³. This extreme baseline represents an atmospheric load more than ten times higher than the World Health Organization’s strict annual safety guideline, which caps safe continuous exposure at merely 5 µg/m³ (WHO, 2023). 
Currently, essentially the entire Indian population resides in geographic districts where baseline annual particulate levels drastically exceed these international safety thresholds. The public health burden generated by this severe exposure is devastating. Recent verified data aggregated in *The Lancet Planetary Health* directly links fine particulate air pollution to approximately 1.7 million premature deaths annually across India. The empirical data explicitly highlights that for every 10 µg/m³ increase in localized PM₂.₅ concentration, corresponding municipal mortality rates surge by an estimated 8.6%, functioning as a massive suppressant on total national life expectancy.

The extraordinarily small size of these particles is the direct mechanical reason behind their high lethality. While the human respiratory system naturally traps large environmental dust in the upper airway, microscopic pollutants easily evade our innate biological defenses. When inhaled, coarse PM₁₀ fragments travel deep into the lower lungs, physically irritating delicate pulmonary tissue. Beyond this devastating physiological cost, the presence of highly concentrated fine particles acts as the absolute primary driver of severe environmental haze. This persistent visual pollution deeply diminishes functional visibility across dense urban commercial centers and profoundly degrades the natural aesthetic condition of the country's treasured national parks and protected wilderness reserves.

PLANT-BIOMONITORS(use from the paper Bioindicators the natural indicator of environmental pollution)
The progressive intensification of urbanization and industrial activity has precipitated a widespread degradation of atmospheric quality, situating airborne particulate matter (PM) as a primary vector for respiratory pathogenesis and ecosystem instability (WHO, 2005). Conventional physicochemical protocols for measuring atmospheric PM and its associated trace metals rely fundamentally on localized, mechanical telemetric sensor networks. While these technical instruments yield high-resolution, temporally precise data, their operational utility is frequently compromised by high deployment costs, restricted spatial interpolation, and an intrinsic inability to quantify the physiological impact of cumulative, synergistic pollutant exposure on biological systems. Consequently, environmental monitoring methodologies have increasingly integrated Phyto-monitoring—the systematic utilization of native and deployed vegetation as passive atmospheric sinks—to capture highly resolved, spatially continuous pollution gradients (Amaral et al., 2025).
At the foundational level, biomonitoring utilizes specific flora, fauna, or microbial communities to assess ecosystem integrity and detect biogeochemical perturbations. Distinguishing itself from mechanical instrumentation that captures discrete, instantaneous atmospheric snapshots, biomonitoring provides integrated, quantitative data reflecting the cumulative and synergistic physiological impacts of multiple environmental stressors over extended chronological scales (Parmar et al., 2016). In the context of atmospheric PM, terrestrial flora function as highly effective bioindicators; their immobility necessitates constant physiological equilibrium with their microclimate, organically converting the plant biomass into a continuous passive sampler. By analysing species-specific tissue for the accumulation of chemical markers, researchers can reconstruct localized atmospheric contamination histories and execute robust spatial source apportionment without the capital constraints of large-scale mechanical sensor arrays (Amaral et al., 2025).
The aerodynamic interception and subsequent retention of PM are physically governed by the target species' morphological architecture. Historically, non-vascular cryptogams such as lichens and bryophytes have served as the foundational standard for atmospheric heavy metal assessment. Lacking subterranean root systems and protective epicuticular waxes, these species depend almost exclusively on ambient atmospheric deposition for nutritional sustenance. This profound physiological exposure, coupled with exceptionally high surface-area-to-volume ratios, renders them highly efficient, passive substrates for aerosol entrapment. For example, (Parmar et al. 2016) detail field applications utilizing the moss Hylocomium splendens to quantitatively trace heavy zinc ore plumes across a 75 km transport corridor. Tissue assays confirmed that heavy metal PM concentrations strictly correlated with spatial proximity to the mechanical emission source, validating the species' capacity to reliably map fugitive industrial dust. Similarly, the localized absence of lichen has long functioned as a definitive diagnostic biomarker for elevated atmospheric sulfur dioxide (SO₂) and nitrogenous trace pollutants (Walsh, 1978).
Contemporary urban biomonitoring, however, relies increasingly on vascular evergreens to act as active multidirectional filtration infrastructure. For complex macrophytes, macroscopic canopy architecture directly dictates initial PM interception efficiency. Coniferous taxa consistently outperform broad-leaved species due to the extreme surface roughness and topological complexity of their needle matrices, which effectively perturb laminar airflow envelopes to induce particulate impaction (Freer-Smith et al., 2003). Longitudinal field arrays emphasize this physiological divergence: under equivalent roadside exposure, the dense geometry of Scots pine (Pinus sylvestris) accumulated capacities up to 417.6 µg cm⁻² of foliar PM, significantly exceeding the smooth, planar canopy limits of common ivy (Hedera helix, maximum 140.6 µg cm⁻²) (Przybysz et al., 2014).
Furthermore, the analytical fidelity of a vascular bio-monitor is mediated by its capacity to permanently immobilize intercepted particulates against hydraulic erosion from precipitation. This retention depends entirely on the epicuticular wax layer, which mechanically partitions accumulated mass into vulnerable, surface-deposited particulate matter (SPM) and permanently wax-embedded particulate matter (WPM). Scots pine foliage extrudes massive epicuticular wax volumes (up to 715.6 µg cm⁻²), forming a viscous matrix capable of deeply embedding incoming aerosolized PM. Conversely, taxa with minimal cuticular extrusion, such as H. helix (maximum 45.2 µg cm⁻²), experience rapid cuticular saturation, rendering surface-bound PM highly susceptible to subsequent rainfall wash-off. Critically, cuticular embedding functions as a permanent, size-selective barrier: heavy precipitation routinely strips 30% to 41% of coarse and large particles (10–100 µm), while the toxicologically critical fine fraction (0.2–2.5 µm) bypasses hydraulic shear entirely, remaining deeply sequestered within the wax (Przybysz et al., 2014). This dynamic establishes heavy-wax species as unparalleled bioindicators for mapping long-term ambient fine particulate exposure.
Despite extensive empirical characterization of independent monitoring mechanisms—ranging from cryptogamic atmospheric diffusion (Parmar et al., 2016) to wax-embedding retention models in evergreen canopies (Przybysz et al., 2014), and controlled herbaceous indicator standardizations (Amaral et al., 2025)—comprehensive baseline data comparing these disparate accumulation paradigms remains structurally fragmented. This lack of standardization inhibits the uniform application of comparative phyto-monitoring across heterogeneous urban and industrial landscapes. Therefore, this study aims to systematically evaluate and compare the absolute particulate matter monitoring potential of several established plant bio-monitors. By rigorously contrasting their macroscopic interception efficiencies, micromorphological partitioning capacities, and precipitation-adjusted retention profiles, this research seeks to standardize foliar accumulation baselines, thereby identifying the optimal candidates for high-density deployment and advancing the operational fidelity of foliar particulate bio-analytics.

Plant species differences in particulate matter accumulation on leaf surfaces. A. Saebo, R. Popek, B. Nawrot, H.M. Hanslin, H. Gawronska, S.W. Gawronski

1.Background
Poor urban air quality continues to pose severe risks to public health, with anthropogenic particulate matter (PM) significantly reducing life expectancy and exacerbating cardiovascular and respiratory conditions (WHO, 2003; Bosko et al., 2005; Bell et al., 2011). Although controlling pollution at its source is essential, deploying urban vegetation serves as a highly effective complementary remediation strategy (Nowak et al., 2006). Numerous studies indicate that integrating trees and woodland patches into cityscapes offers a cost-efficient method to intercept airborne contaminants, leading to measurable local reductions in PM load (Escobedo et al., 2008; Cavanagh et al., 2009; Yin et al., 2011; Yang et al., 2005). The structural complexity of tree canopies naturally promotes air turbulence, which, combined with specific leaf surface traits, facilitates the deposition and retention of floating particles (Fowler et al., 1989; Burkhardt, 2010).
Despite this established potential, current urban planting strategies are hindered by a critical knowledge gap regarding species-specific mitigation performance. While it is broadly understood that broad-leaved plants with rough foliage or certain evergreens are adept at trapping pollutants, there remains a shortage of comparative data explicitly measuring PM deposition across different plant species—especially concerning the most hazardous fine particulate fractions below 2.5 µm (Beckett et al., 2000; Hwang et al., 2011). Selecting optimal urban vegetation requires balancing high PM capture efficiency with plant resilience to urban stressors, while also accounting for potential negative trade-offs like the emission of biogenic volatile organic compounds (BVOCs) (Chappelka and Freer-Smith, 1995; Simpson and McPherson, 2011). Systematic, empirical research documenting the exact PM accumulation capabilities of various common taxa is therefore necessary. Establishing this comparative baseline is a prerequisite for identifying the most efficient species, ultimately allowing planners to design targeted, high-performance green infrastructure that actively reduces human exposure to anthropogenic emissions (Dzierzanowski et al., 2011; Escobedo et al., 2011).
2.Study Area
The research was conducted concurrently at two distinct test sites in Europe, selected deliberately to expose the identical plant species to contrasting environmental conditions and baseline pollution levels over a two-year tracking period.
High-Exposure Site: Stavanger, Norway The Norwegian monitoring site actively subjected the test plants to heavy localized traffic emissions. It was established in a test field situated directly next to a busy, high-volume motorway outside Stavanger, Norway (Coordinates: 58°47′N, 5°41′E). The vegetation specimens at this location were planted directly into the ground. During the typical May to September growing seasons spanning the test years, average air temperatures fluctuated between 13.6°C and 15.1°C, while total seasonal precipitation ranged from 448 mm to 568 mm.
Low-Exposure Site: Rural Poland The Polish baseline site served as a low-exposure environmental contrast. It was established on undeveloped, open plant nursery land situated in a rural geographic zone specifically chosen for its strict isolation from heavy urban traffic and industrial centres (Coordinates: 52°52′N, 20°34′E). Unlike the Norwegian cohort, the vegetation here was grown in pots and maintained via automated drip irrigation systems. Over the extended April to October growing periods during the study, this rural site recorded steady average temperatures of 13.6°C, with total seasonal precipitation levels spanning 376 mm to 511 mm.
3.Materials and methods
To establish a rigorous comparative baseline of biological monitoring potential, (Saebo et al. 2012) evaluated a diverse cohort of 47 widely used woody taxa, explicitly comprising 22 tree and 25 shrub species. To ensure geographical validity and verify whether species-specific accumulation rankings remained stable under varying atmospheric loads, the researchers established experimental trials at two contrasting test sites: a high-exposure zone adjacent to a highly trafficked motorway in Norway, and a low-exposure, clean rural nursery in Poland. Over two complete growing seasons, four replicate plants per species were randomly distributed within the respective plots. Structurally sound, mature leaves were harvested simultaneously at the end of the vegetative cycle, intentionally timed immediately prior to the autumn drop of deciduous leaves to ensure the sample data represented the maximum seasonal accumulation phase.
To extract and isolate the necessary particulate fractions, (Saebo et al. 2012) employed a highly specific, sequential gravimetric washing protocol adapted from the methodology outlined by (Dzierzanowski et al. 2011). This separation technique successfully differentiated the distinct entrapment mechanisms utilized by different plant monitors. Foliage was initially agitated in a water bath to isolate loosely deposited surface particulate matter (SPM). Following this, specimens were subjected to a chloroform rinse designed to solubilize the entire epicuticular wax layer, thereby releasing the particle fraction permanently immobilized within the waxes (WPM).
The accumulated washings—representing particulates captured on both the adaxial (upper) and abaxial (lower) leaf surfaces—were processed through a progressive cascade of specialized Whatman filters. This sequence (utilizing Type 91 for 10 µm retention, Type 42 for 2.5 µm, and a specialized PTFE membrane for 0.2 µm) cleanly isolated three specific aerodynamic mass fractions: large (PM10), fine (PM2.5), and ultrafine (PM0.2). To preserve microgram-level accuracy, all filters were neutralized by a standard deionizer before weighing to eliminate electrostatic fluctuations. Absolute mass returns for all fractions were subsequently mathematically normalized per unit of continuous leaf area via digital image scanning. This allowed the authors to calculate critical performance metrics for each species, most notably the direct ratio of unbound surface particulates against wax-encapsulated particulates.
To delineate exactly why certain species excel as biomonitors, the study conducted comprehensive leaf surface profiling across the test groups. General morphological parameters, including specific leaf area (SLA) and overall individual leaf dry weight, were calculated empirically by the team. Crucially, utilizing 6–12x microscopic magnification, researchers assigned quantitative structural scores (on a specialized 1 to 5 visual scale) detailing both foliar trichome (hair) density and overall topographical roughness across both leaf sides.
By integrating this morphometric profiling with the multi-fraction gravimetric data through analysis of variance (ANOVA) and multiple regression modelling,(Saebo et al. 2012) mapped the precise influence of leaf topography and wax quantity on particle accumulation capabilities. Ultimately, utilizing standardized cross-seasonal accumulation averages, K-means cluster analysis was applied to definitively categorize the 47 analysed species into mathematically discrete high, intermediate, and low accumulation tiers, explicitly ranking their comparative utility as biological monitors for municipal planning.
4.Result and Discussion

Executing a rigorous comparison of particulate matter (PM) monitoring potential among established plant biomonitors necessitates fully acknowledging the vast volumetric scale of atmospheric pollution these species intercept. Baseline municipal interception models document staggeringly high mass retention volumes; urban woodlands in Beijing capture approximately 772 tons of PM10 annually (Yang et al., 2005), while nationwide U.S. urban vegetation intercepts an estimated 215,000 tons of the PM10 fraction over equivalent periods (Nowak et al., 2006). Spatial dispersal studies in Shanghai confirmed that utilizing continuous forest patches triggered acute local concentration reductions by 9.1% for PM, 5.3% for SO2, and 2.6% for NO2 at boundary penetrations of 50 to 100 meters (Yin et al., 2011). Because utilizing vegetation operates as a massive passive filtration network, identifying and standardizing the precise biological monitors responsible for these local reductions via empirical surface testing is a critical environmental necessity.
To systematically benchmark tracking capabilities across a standardized 47-species cohort (composed exactly of 22 tree and 25 shrub taxa), (Saebo et al. 2012) engineered a dual-site physiological baseline designed to test biomonitoring stability under fluctuating climatic extremes and absolute pollution loads. Across two growing seasons, generating n=8 independent biological replicates per species, the experimental parameters incorporated sharp regional contrasts. Evaluating wash-off vulnerability required subjecting taxa to variable precipitation patterns: the high-exposure Norwegian motorway site sustained 568 mm of precipitation spanning 2009 and 448 mm in 2010 alongside average temperatures shifting from 15.1±3.8°C to 13.6±4.1°C, while the cleaner Polish test site countered with 376 mm jumping aggressively to 511 mm against stable 13.6°C mean temperatures.
Evaluating the geometric mean deposition under these exact climatic disparities exposed extreme biological monitoring stratification. Applying cross-seasonal K-means mathematical clustering separated species into rigid taxonomic performance tiers maintaining highly distinct numerical gulfs. In the high-pollution Norwegian setting, elite taxa in highest cluster (Betula pendula, Pinus mugo, Pinus sylvestris, Salix cinerea, Skimmia japonica, and Stephanandra incisa) sustained immense two-year mean gross accumulation volumes ranging between 24 and 55 μg cm−2 of total PM per valid leaf unit. Conversely, structurally inefficient biomonitors fixed in the poorest performing cluster—encompassing Acer platanoides, Prunus avium, Prunus laurocerasus, Prunus padus, Symphoricarpos albus, and Tilia cordata—yielded radically depressed baseline capture data restricted to merely 6 to 13 μg cm−2. Even within the vastly reduced ambient pollution envelope of the Polish site, basic genetic predisposition overrode location; B. pendula operated mathematically isolated within the highest tier, sequestering 38.4 μg cm−2 of total captured PM and conclusively proving its overarching utility as an apex environmental tracking species.

Measuring cumulative bulk mass severely obscures the capture rates for the most hazardous sub-micron aerodynamic fractions. Isolating the PM0.2 metric generated highly specific biomonitoring differentials. Elite Norwegian tracking targets (B. pendula, P. mugo, S. incisa) heavily dominated ultrafine retention, capturing specific baseline mass pools between 4.2 and 8.0 μg cm−2. At the opposite efficiency pole, poor broadleaved monitors (Fagus silvatica, A. platanoides, T. cordata) secured fundamentally irrelevant ultrafine capture yields stagnating at 1.0 to 1.6 μg cm−2. The Polish site similarly restricted elite sub-micron entrapment (1.4 to 4.5 μg cm−2) to specialized taxa including B. pendula, Taxus baccata, and Alnus spaethii, while baseline municipal trees like Fraxinus excelsior collapsed entirely, failing to scale past 0.6 to 0.8 μg cm−2.
Because monitoring potential demands permanent sequestration rather than temporary surface resting, analyzing the exact ratio of loosely deposited surface particulate matter (SPM) against internally immobilized wax particulate matter (WPM) establishes a monitor's terminal capability. F. silvatica registered an operationally vulnerable surface-to-wax anomaly of 3.2 ± 1.3, empirically demonstrating that approximately 75% of its intercepted PM load lingered on the unprotected epidermis, exposed to imminent wash-off. Contrastingly, B. pendula optimized a highly secure 0.2 ± 0.1 ratio, safely incarcerating 82.6% of its accumulated mass deeply within its lipophilic matrix. The Polish Hedera helix cohort manifested a catastrophic failure ratio marked by a 10.8 ± 5.3 divergence exclusively for PM0.2 fractions, completely violating the balanced 0.7 to 2.9 mass retention ratios successfully achieved by allied intermediate taxa.
Determining the cellular architecture underpinning these massive data variations required extracting partial regression coefficients (β). The regression outputs compiled by Sæbø et al. (2012) confirmed empirically why specific plants operate as superior indicators. Total epicuticular wax volume operated as the overwhelmingly dominant universal indicator; regression results generated high positive Beta coefficients driving Total PM (β = 0.66, p = 0.000), PM10 (β = 0.59, p = 0.001), and the critical PM0.2 boundary fraction (β = 0.78, p = 0.000). The specific R² explanatory variance for ultrafine particles alone hit 0.55, verifying that resolving sub-micron contamination fundamentally requires the continuous internal lipophilic encapsulation process occurring chemically within plant waxes (Burkhardt, 2010).
Analyzing structural topography yielded highly disparate predictive metrics. The presence of dense foliar trichomes (hairs) produced statistically significant capture escalations for larger geometric classes, securing a positive relationship against Total PM (β = 0.31, p = 0.003) and PM2.5 (β = 0.45, p = 0.035). However, evaluating the sub-micron PM0.2 fraction against trichome density collapsed entirely (β = 0.20, generating a statistically void p = 0.185). This data isolates an aggressive biological reality: trichomes physically screen large atmospheric detritus, but dense chemical waxes are uniquely required to process bio-hazardous ultrafine pollution. Additionally, specific leaf area (SLA) maintained heavy negative correlations scaling from β = -0.34 to -0.36 (p = 0.002) against aggregate particulate loads, indicating inflated leaf expansion limits aerodynamic boundary drag efficiencies. Macroscopic surface roughness (wrinkling) returned negligible coefficients across the board (β = -0.01 to -0.08, matching useless p-values from 0.591 to 0.91), quantitatively disproving prevailing theories prioritizing heavily textured leaf biology for primary air monitoring applications (Hwang et al., 2011).

The regression dataset failed to fully account for the extraordinary capture thresholds executed by coniferous species. Despite naturally lacking the critical trichome structures statistically tied to macroscopic particle interception, Pinus varieties mirrored the densest hairy broadleaves in total geometric yield. Consequently, the specialized three-dimensional spatial architectures created by dense needle bundlings evidently forge an elite aerodynamic filtration labyrinth fully capable of bypassing standard surface-interception metrics. Simultaneously, the robust 24 to 55 μg cm−2 data pulled from low-growing shrubs, most notably S. incisa, forces an operational re-evaluation of lower-canopy sampling. Anchored immediately adjacent to soil surfaces, these organisms intercept direct resuspension loads bypassing upper canopy environments entirely, securing their role as pivotal lower-strata tracking nodes (Dzierzanowski et al., 2011).
Executing reliable multi-scale environmental indexing strictly requires standardizing metrics down to the single leaf scale geometry rather than executing whole-tree gross tonnage calculations. Extrapolating the severe dangers of generic complete-canopy profiling, volumetric studies conducted in Florence cataloged whole-tree capture variances plunging from 182 g tree−1 (Aesculus hippocastanum) straightforwardly down to a minuscule 2.6 g tree−1 (Carpinus betulus) simply due to immense structural and age-related canopy disparities (Paoletti et al., 2011). However, when evaluated rigorously on an equalized square centimeter leaf-area baseline by (Saebo et al. 2012), C. betulus and A. hippocastanum registered nearly identical accumulation capabilities. Bypassing uncontrolled localized soil, climate, and age limitations demands environmental chemists uniformly adopt the strict measurement of actual geometric deposition per squared unit of valid monitoring tissue, stripping away generic full-tree taxonomic assumptions (Freer-Smith et al., 2005).
5.Conclusion
Analysing 47 distinct tree and shrub species revealed accumulation differences spanning a 10- to 20-fold range. These variations confirm that the capacity to intercept and retain particulate matter is fundamentally species-dependent. Utilizing randomly selected municipal vegetation for environmental monitoring leads to inefficient assessment models. For instance, taxa presenting poor capture metrics, such as Acer platanoides, maintained minimal accumulation baselines of just 6 to 13 μg cm−2. Under identical atmospheric exposure, highly effective taxonomic candidates proved capable of sustaining geometric loads between 24 and 55 μg cm−2.
Regression modelling clarifies these discrepancies by isolating specific morphological drivers. Foliar trichomes proved highly capable of intercepting coarser geometric particles. However, total epicuticular wax volume functioned as the primary physiological requirement for trapping hazardous sub-micron components. Wax content generated a β coefficient of 0.78 and independently accounted for 55% of the total mass variance associated with the PM0.2 boundary. This high wax threshold enables internal chemical sequestration. High-wax bio-monitors can permanently encapsulate pollutants within their lipophilic layers—achieving surface-to-wax entrapment ratios as low as 0.2—rather than leaving the mass vulnerable to rapid weather wash-off on the unprotected leaf epidermis.
Integrating this morphometric data provides a practical framework for isolating specific plant candidates for municipal biological assessment networks. Where ground-level monitoring is required, assessment protocols should prioritize high-capacity woody shrubs like Stephanandra incisa, Pinus mugo, Skimmia japonica, and Salix cinerea. For mapping upper-canopy airspace, Pinus sylvestris and Betula pendula exhibit the structural and chemical profiles necessary to yield stable, reliable collection data across fluctuating urban pollution gradients.

Particulate matter deposition on roadside plants and the importance of leaf trait combinations. Ryan J. Leonard, Clare McArthur, Dieter F. Hochuli
1.Background
Urban environments face persistent atmospheric degradation, primarily driven by vehicular emissions that release particulate matter heavily bound with hazardous heavy metals and polyaromatic hydrocarbons. The continuous inhalation of these specific toxic fractions correlates directly with severe respiratory and cardiovascular morbidities (Polichetti et al., 2009; Ram et al., 2015; Vu et al., 2015). While mechanical emission controls remain necessary, intercepting these specific aerosols via established urban vegetation networks serves as a highly viable biological filtration mechanism capable of actively removing accumulated toxics from the immediate atmospheric boundary (Escobedo and Nowak, 2009; Popek et al., 2013).
Optimizing the actual field deployment of these biological monitors, however, requires resolving clear deficits in current environmental deposition modelling. While isolated physiological features—such as extensive foliar pubescence, deep epicuticular wax layering, superficial epidermal roughness, and macroscopic canopy density—are individually recognized for increasing aerodynamic particle entrapment (Mo et al., 2015; Nowak et al., 2006), massive data deficits exist regarding how complex combinations of these distinct traits interact structurally across widely varying taxa to influence absolute capture efficiency. Furthermore, because the active entrapment of metallic and atmospheric layers induces direct localized toxicity within the monitoring foliage itself—frequently triggering severe stomatal failure, photosynthetic inhibition, and accelerated cellular senescence (Daresta et al., 2015; Rai, 2016)—identifying resilient species capable of accurately tracking high urban pollution loads without experiencing physiological collapse is a fundamental requirement for sustainable infrastructure. Consequently, empirical research explicitly quantifying elemental deposition capacities across heavily cultivated roadside indicator species is biologically necessary. Executing this precise morphological profiling establishes the quantitative baseline required to determine exactly which structural trait combinations yield the highest long-term biomonitoring potential under severe vehicular exposure.
2.Sudy Area
The experimental field sampling was conducted in Australia, specifically targeting eight localized test sites distributed throughout the Greater Sydney Region in New South Wales. The geographical monitoring boundaries were established directly alongside moderately to heavily utilized, flat arterial roadways maintaining active traffic speed limits between 60 and 70 kilometres per hour.
3.Materials and Method
To establish a comparative baseline for urban biological monitoring, field sampling was conducted across eight distinct roadside locations within the Greater Sydney Region. Site selection was strictly limited to flat topographies adjacent to roadways supporting 60 to 70 km/h traffic, with minimum road widths exceeding 10 meters. The study monitored 16 native taxa. Sampling was standardized to the final week of October, November, and December 2012 to accurately track temporal accumulation trends. Depending on localized site density, between two and six individual plants per species were permanently tagged. Where target populations were highly abundant, subjects were selected using a randomized *n*th-nearest spatial approach to prevent selection bias (Barbour et al., 1987). Collecting exclusively from individuals situated within two meters of the carriageway, researchers harvested terminal shoots stationed at a uniform 1.5-meter breast height—a necessary control against known vertical particulate stratification gradients (Mitchell et al., 2010). A minimum viable sample size of 30 intact leaves, harvested strictly between the second and eighth internodes, was established. Samples were immediately refrigerated in moisture-controlled plastic bags prior to extraction.
Unlike protocols designed to evaluate permanent internal wax encapsulation, this extraction methodology exclusively targeted loosely bound surface deposits simulating natural wash-off during standard precipitation. Foliage underwent exactly 60 seconds of mechanical agitation in reverse osmosis water, intentionally leaving deeper epicuticular particulate fractions intact on the leaf epidermis (Dzierzanowski et al., 2011). The resultant fluid was forced through a 100 μm mesh sieve before entering a dual-stage Buchner vacuum filtration queue. Suspended mass was captured sequentially on Whatman grade 541 (22 μm) and grade GF/A (1.6 μm) filters, successfully generating distinct 'large' (22–100 μm) and 'small' (1.6–22 μm) aerodynamic fractions. Filters underwent strict thermal baseline calibrations—involving 100°C oven drying and prolonged ambient desiccation—both before and after fluid processing to guarantee precise gravimetric yields. Final mass values were mathematically constrained directly against absolute leaf surface area, which was digitally mapped using ImageJ software parameters operating on methods established by (Osunkoya et al. 2010). All specimens were subsequently morphologically categorized by general habit, geometric arrangement, gross shape, and trichome (hair) presence.
To quantify the specific accumulation of vehicular trace metals, heavily loaded filter papers were chemically degraded utilizing a dedicated nitric and hydrochloric acid reflux protocol. The resulting acidic extracts were analyzed via inductively coupled plasma-atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES) operating under established United States Environmental Protection Agency guidelines (USEPA Method 200.8; USEPA, 1994). While the spectroscopic array initially targeted a broad spectrum of traffic-associated elements (Herngren and Goonetilleke, 2006), detection limits restricted reliable quantification strictly to copper, chromium, manganese, aluminium, and iron. This specific elemental profiling was executed on *Acacia parramattensis* and *Acacia longifolia* tissue collected across three isolated sites.
Standard parametric assumptions failed due to severely non-normal gravimetric data distributions; consequently, physiological trait influences and overall species capture efficiencies were evaluated using robust Kruskal–Wallis continuous matrices (Field, 2009). Associated significant variances were parsed utilizing Dunn’s pairwise post-hoc tests, while the specific structural impact of foliar trichomes was isolated using targeted Mann-Whitney U testing. Deposition variability across the 90-day tracking window was modelled via Friedman’s non-parametric analysis of variance (Field, 2009). Finally, to contrast the distinct chemical profiles returned by the ICP-AES platform, multiple independent 1-way ANOVAs were executed. This independent approach—reinforced by severe Bonferroni corrections (adjusted P < 0.01)—was specifically chosen over a unified multivariate model to bypass the inherent statistical skewing associated with heavy metal multicollinearity (Quinn and Keough, 2002).
4.Result and Discussion
Deposition loads varied strongly across the 16 species evaluated (H15 = 82.68, P < 0.001). Morphology dictated capture rates. Basic leaf shape drove significant differences in deposition (H4 = 28.08, P < 0.001), with the lanceolate profile outperforming linear, elliptic, and needle-like structures. Foliar trichomes also provided a mechanical advantage. Leaves with hairs returned significantly heavier loads than bald leaves (U = 4295, P = 0.03). Evaluating precise taxa makes this hierarchy clear. The hairy, lanceolate shrub *Westringia fruticosa* (sampled strictly across 6 sites) trapped more mass than multiple competitors, including *Acacia longifolia* and *Banksia spinulosa*.
Other physical traits failed to influence accumulation. Differentiating between shrubs (returning 3.88 ± 1.15 mg cm⁻²) and trees (returning 3.00 ± 0.68 mg cm⁻²) produced no statistical variance (P = 0.58). Leaf arrangement was similarly irrelevant. Testing showed opposite alignments collecting just 0.56 ± 0.32 mg cm⁻² and alternate alignments collecting 1.78 ± 0.28 mg cm⁻². Whorled setups generated higher means hitting 6.33 ± 3.95 mg cm⁻², but extreme internal variance rendered the effect statistically insignificant (P = 0.14). Result levels also remained completely stable over the three-month sampling window (P = 0.49).
Understanding these outcomes points directly to mechanical leaf movement under stress. Lanceolate leaves typically hold an aerodynamic advantage over elliptic or obovate foliage. Narrow leaf bases elevate specific drag, causing leaves to flutter erratically in wind streams (Vogel, 1989). This fluttering physically shakes loose deposited material before it can permanently stick. We see this limitation actively operating in species possessing long petioles exceeding 1 cm combined with large surface areas. *Persoonia levis* and *Dodonaea triquetra* fit this profile entirely and consistently retained far less mass than stiff, short-petioled species like *Melaleuca styphelioides* (which generated the third-highest total capture rate overall).
Traits do not operate in a vacuum. Leaf hairs usually improve retention by expanding the boundary surface and preventing dislodgment (Neinhuis and Barthlott, 1998; Qiu et al., 2009). However, conflicting traits can suppress this advantage. *Acacia longifolia* possesses dense hairs but accumulated significantly less mass than the completely smooth-leaved *Allocasuarina littoralis*. Deep epicuticular wax on *A. longifolia* likely establishes a hydrophobic barrier that limits initial particle adhesion, ultimately overriding the expected physical benefit of the foliar hairs (Faini et al., 1999). *Hakea salicifolia* shares the optimal lanceolate shape but yielded very poor retention. This proves that stomatal density or glabrous surfaces sometimes dictate the final capacity of the monitor regardless of favourable geometry (Mo et al., 2015). Complex interactions like these explain why the data here contradicts comparative studies from Southeast Asia. (Chaturvedi et al. 2013) claimed whorled leaf arrangements inherently maximize baseline capture volume, but severe mass variance localized on the whorled *Banksia integrifolia* in this study negated that generalized topological rule.
Trace metal accumulation was evaluated across *A. parramattensis* and *A. longifolia*. Chromium levels differed significantly (P = 0.05). *A. parramattensis* held 0.51 ± 0.11 mg kg⁻¹ compared to a fractional 0.09 ± 0.04 mg kg⁻¹ in *A. longifolia*. Copper, manganese, aluminium, and iron remained statistically static between the paired species. Copper returned massive internal variance, hitting 4.49 ± 3.82 mg kg⁻¹ for *A. longifolia* but just 1.21 ± 0.53 mg kg⁻¹ on *A. parramattensis* (P = 0.49). Manganese loads sat below 1.0 mg kg⁻¹ across the board.
It is absolutely necessary to view these profiles against background atmospheric load data. Total PM volumes assessed here matched global studies. Chaturvedi et al. (2013) reported general bounds of 4 mg cm⁻² for *Syzygium cumini* and 7 mg cm⁻² for *Tectona grandis*. This mirrors the 0.5 to 6.3 mg cm⁻² ranges recorded on the Sydney taxa. Despite this geometric mass equilibrium, the immediate heavy metal fractions sit far beneath global thresholds. Typical plant monitoring near arterial roads in China routinely isolates copper loads exceeding 4000 mg kg⁻¹ (Zheng et al., 2013). This local suppression highlights Australia’s comparatively clean baseline air quality. (Hopke et al. 2008) track Australia PM10 averages at just 10.49 ± 7.47 μg m⁻³. In contrast, equivalent regions in China average 109.29 ± 56.47 μg m⁻³ for PM10, and India averages 82.83 ± 33.01 μg m⁻³. Looking past vehicular heavy metals entirely, the Australian leaves locally trapped massive volumes of elemental aluminium (249–300 mg kg⁻¹) and iron (16–63 mg kg⁻¹). These act as primary mineral markers for crushed road base aggregates and disturbed soil (Herngren and Goonetilleke, 2006). Locating these elements in such extreme concentrations suggests that mechanical road dust resuspension currently provides the bulk of the immediate PM load on these bio-monitors, rather than direct tailpipe emissions or dense industrial fallout.
5.Conclusion
Analysis of these native bio-monitors confirms that urban vegetation actively sequesters atmospheric particulate matter sourced directly from localized traffic corridors. Chemical profiling of the accumulated mass verifies that the foliar deposits originate predominantly from vehicular exhaust, physical tire degradation, and mechanically resuspended ground soils. Exploiting this natural aerodynamic filtration mechanism offers a scalable biological remediation strategy for densely populated municipalities suffering from traffic-induced air quality failures.
While specific structural traits—namely lanceolate leaf geometries and dense epidermal trichomes—heavily drive particulate collection efficiency, their mechanical advantages are frequently neutralized by conflicting physiological features like deep epicuticular wax layers. Actively selecting flora for environmental monitoring therefore demands a holistic assessment of interacting plant trait combinations rather than relying on isolated morphological markers. The quantitative data established here secures a functional biological baseline for subsequent research, enabling deeper evaluation into how sustained heavy metal loads physically stress the bio-monitors themselves, and determining which exact structural combinations ultimately maximize urban atmospheric remediation.

1.Background
Anthropogenic particulate matter (PM) emissions from industrial processes and transport vectors constitute a primary global health risk, contributing to an estimated 8.8 million annual mortalities (Lelieveld et al., 2020). As sustained global urbanization projects shift an additional 2.5 billion individuals into active metropolitan boundaries by 2050 (UN, 2018), aggregate human exposure to hazardous baseline PM concentrations will escalate severely. To mitigate this exposure, municipal planners increasingly deploy urban green infrastructure (GI)—integrated networks of street trees, understory shrubs, green roofs, and living walls—as an active environmental abatement mechanism (McDonald et al., 2007; Mo et al., 2015). 
Vegetation arrays perform structural abatement by altering localized aerodynamic flow patterns and actively capturing atmospheric pollutants through stomatal absorption of gases and the direct surface adsorption of suspended particulates (Dzierzanowski et al., 2011; Nowak et al., 2018). This specific accumulation occurs via two distinct physical pathways. Dry deposition relies entirely on gravitational sedimentation and turbulent aerodynamic impaction, heavily favoring the capture of coarse fractions like PM10. Conversely, wet deposition engages ambient atmospheric moisture—such as precipitation or dense fog—to bind and precipitate finer suspended elements like PM2.5 through physical mass and adherence effects before they strike the foliage (Wu et al., 2018). 
Theoretical modeling of these interactions suggests that established municipal canopies possess immense regional filtration capacity; baseline estimates project that the larger London urban forest extracts between 800 and 2000 tonnes of PM10 annually (Tallis et al., 2011). These high-yield models directly drive heavy municipal investment in mass-planting initiatives across major global cities (Campbell et al., 2014). However, severe empirical data gaps regarding target efficiency heavily compromise these large-scale GI installations. The physical mechanics controlling high-volume particulate removal remain strictly reliant on species-specific physiological variables. Despite the rapid expansion of municipal greening, civil planning strategies still operate without definitive, holistic baselines capable of identifying which specific foliar properties—such as target trichome distribution, epicuticular wax density, physical surface topology, and aerodynamic plant geometry—actually maximize trace particulate deposition in high-stress urban zones.

3.Materals and Method
To evaluate the biological mechanics of particulate mitigation, (Corada et al. 2021) conducted a systematic literature review synthesizing quantitative field data on optimal leaf traits for particulate matter (PM) capture across street trees, green walls, and green roofs. Using the PRISMA framework (Moher et al., 2009), the authors searched four databases (Scopus, Web of Science, ScienceDirect, Google Scholar) for English-language, peer-reviewed studies published between 2000 and 2019. Strict exclusion operators were applied to eliminate non-empirical research, including predictive modelling, stormwater management, and urban heat island studies.
Rigorous eligibility gating mandated that underlying studies provide primary, physical field measurements of PM deposited directly onto the foliage of identifiable urban plant species, complete with extractable summary statistics linked specifically to foliar traits. This strict filtration reduced an initial scoping pool of 409 candidate papers into a final corpus of 62 eligible measurement studies. The resulting field data originated primarily from two geographic clusters: Asia contributed the majority of the studies (n = 34, predominantly China: n = 29), while Europe provided a secondary data pool (n = 25, led by the UK: n = 7, and Poland: n = 5).
A critical finding of the review was the severe methodological fragmentation across the biomonitoring field, a factor that actively prohibited a standard mathematical meta-analysis of exact PM concentrations. The final 62 studies relied on fundamentally incompatible particulate isolation and quantification techniques: gravimetric mass extraction was the primary methodology (n = 32), followed by visual particle counting via Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) (n = 13), Saturation Isothermal Remanent Magnetization (SIRM) (n = 7), and isolated wind tunnel testing (n = 5). Sampling parameters were equally disparate, with collection heights ranging from 0.01 m to 12 m (modal height: 1.5–2.0 m). Furthermore, ambient geographic baselines were rarely quantified; only 12 studies utilized dedicated background PM monitoring stations, while a vast majority (n = 40) substituted numerical baselines with subjective, qualitative site descriptions (e.g., proximity to active motorways). 
Confronted by incompatible unit systems (mass scaling versus particulate counts) and disjointed target parameters (adaxial versus abaxial surfaces; surface-adhered versus wax-encapsulated particulates), (Corada et al). successfully synthesized the research by establishing a citation-frequency index. This alternative analytical framework evaluated overall leaf trait efficacy indirectly by quantifying exactly how often specific foliar structures were empirically validated within the literature as the primary physical drivers of high or low particulate accumulation.
4.Reslt and Discussion
The capacity of specific vegetation to serve as functional biological monitors for atmospheric particulate matter (PM) is determined by a strict matrix of microscopic foliar traits, macroscopic plant geometry, and isolated environmental variables. Evaluating the relative monitoring potential between species requires calculating these converging biological and physical factors. 
Systematic evaluation identifies foliar trichome density as the primary mechanical driver for maximizing both coarse and fine (PM2.5) particulate deposition. Trichomatic structures actively disrupt aerodynamic boundary layer resistance across the foliar plane, forcing impaction (Bakker et al., 1999; Ridge, 2002; Vadlamani et al., 2017). Species characterized by dense adaxial trichomes register significantly higher metric baseline accumulations than glabrous control species or those exhibiting isolated abaxial pubescence (Saebo et al., 2012; Chen et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2017). 
Macro-topological roughness directly correlates with elevated coarse fraction (PM10) retention. Total bioaccumulation scales in direct mathematical proportion to epidermal groove density and furrow width, with heavily rigid, wrinkled surfaces vastly outperforming smooth planes (Blanusa et al., 2015; Leonard et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2018). At the microscopic level, species presenting large, highly dense stomatal arrays exhibit superior capture potentials for ultrafine fractions, successfully isolating trace particulates deeply within internal stomatic cavities (Rasanen et al., 2013; Song et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2015).
Epicuticular wax performs a critical stabilization function by permanently adsorbing or physically encapsulating deposited dust, thereby neutralizing ambient resuspension risk (Hofman et al., 2014; Song et al., 2015). Interestingly, capture efficiency is not strictly dependent on total wax volume; specific thin-wax structures frequently record higher stable deposition levels than uniform thick-wax matrices. This discrepancy indicates that the unique molecular composition and resulting interfacial adhesion physics of the specific wax dictates monitoring efficiency far more than raw physical thickness (Wang et al., 2015; Leonard et al., 2016). 
At a systemic canopy scale, lanceolate, ovate, and acicular (needle) leaf geometries consistently report the highest quantitative capture yields, whereas expanded palmate morphologies universally return the lowest accumulation volumes (Saebo et al., 2012; Mo et al., 2015; Leonard et al., 2016). Coniferous evergreens generally assert the highest overall biomonitoring potential due to their complex multi-dimensional spatial geometry, heavy epicuticular adhesion profiles, and mandatory uninterrupted year-round exposure to winter pollution peaks (Beckett et al., 2000). 
The data fidelity of any foliar bio-monitor is heavily moderated by localized precipitation physics, which physically strip accumulated matter from the target surface. Standard rainfall tracks remove between 28% and 70% of the total deposited PM load from an exposed leaf (Przybysz et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2015). The severity of this data wash-off is mediated directly by the structural wettability of the specific leaf. Heavily textured or trichome-dense foliage specifically requires high-intensity, short-duration storm kinetics to structurally dislodge particulate mass; conversely, entirely smooth biological surfaces suffer severe wash-off from merely low-intensity, long-duration drizzle events that increase water-contact time (Wang et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2019). Overall, baseline deciduous broadleaves shed between 51% and 70% of their accumulated PM load during standard rain events, rendering their data highly volatile. In contrast, structurally complex coniferous needles retain the vast majority of their captured load, surrendering only 30% to 41% of their mass to precipitation (Przybysz et al., 2014; Xu et al., 2017). 
The structural implementation format of the monitoring vegetation physically dictates the captured particulate profile. Standard street tree installations function as the primary atmospheric sink for gravitational sedimentation, accumulating heavy coarse particulates (>10 µm) across both their expansive foliar surfaces and their roughened woody cambium structures (Popek et al., 2015; Xu et al., 2019). Alternatively, high-density green wall installations utilizing durable, shrub-form species (e.g., *Juniperus chinensis*, *Berberis* sp.) frequently intercept massive continuous quantities of fine and ultrafine fractions due to their strict vertical orientation acting as a blunt barrier against lateral street-level horizontal airflows (Sternberg et al., 2010; Weerakkody et al., 2018). Finally, specific graminoids deployed in green roof arrays, such as *Agrostis stolonifera* and *Festuca rubra*, demonstrate a markedly higher PM10 capture efficiency per discrete unit of leaf area than comparative broadleaf tree installations (Currie and Bass, 2008; Speak et al., 2012).
5.Conclusion
The systematic aggregation of available field data confirms that relying on isolated foliar morphological traits is biologically insufficient for selecting functional, highly optimized particulate biomonitors. While specific structural criteria—such as acicular (needle) leaf geometries, dense surface trichomes, advanced epicuticular wax profiles, and pronounced macroscopic epidermal roughness—consistently correlate with maximized particulate deposition, no single biological trait operates as a universal catalyst for absolute capture efficiency. Notably, the high-performance capture traits identified across the primary literature intersect heavily with the fundamental physiological adaptations required for baseline botanical drought tolerance, indicating a direct functional overlap between active urban stress resistance and passive particulate filtration capacity.
To deploy reliable biomonitoring grids, field applications demand a holistic structural assessment that transcends simple leaf-level metrics. The entire three-dimensional architecture of the biological subject functions as an active atmospheric sink. Specifically, the rough woody cambium structures spanning mature trunks and internal branch networks serve as primary gravimetric sedimentation zones. These structural components actively sequester massive, continuous quantities of coarse particulate fractions (>10 µm) completely independent of foliar capture mechanisms (Xu et al., 2019). Consequently, optimal species selection must prioritize total macroscopic canopy architecture operating in direct synergy with isolated micro-morphological leaf advantages.
Ultimately, the severe lack of methodological standardization across global field tracking physically prevents the immediate establishment of an absolute, universal hierarchy of plant bio-monitors. The current academic data pool is also geographically skewed; the vast majority of existing trait-efficacy baseline data originates almost exclusively from localized endemic species evaluated within Chinese municipalities. Translating these findings into functional, global biomonitoring protocols requires standardizing exact particulate isolation assays and enforcing strict, mandatory tracking of localized ambient meteorological parameters—specifically precipitation intensity and definitive baseline aerosol concentrations. Until strict universal quantification protocols neutralize these compounding environmental variables, municipal species selection for particulate monitoring must rely on integrated, multi-variable canopy assessments rather than isolated foliar trait targeting.

3.1. The selected species included Magnolia grandiflora, Osmanthus fragrans, Ligustrun lucidum, Cinnamomum camphora, Cinnamomum japonicum, Photinia glomerata, Prunus majestica, Prunus cerasifera f. atropurpurea, Platanus acerifolia, Celtis kunmingensis, Euonymus japonica, Loropetalum chinense, and Rhododendron pulchrum.
All selected biological specimens were at a mid-maturity growth stage and rigorously pre-screened to ensure an absence of pathogenic infections or pre-existing pollution-induced necrosis prior to planting. The field deployment utilized a randomized block distribution across the three functional zones (Industrial, Traffic, and University Campus), with three independent spatial repetitions maintained per zone to account for localized micro-variations. 3.2. Foliar Sample Collection Protocol To accurately capture temporal variations, physical sampling was executed across two distinct growth seasons (June and September 2016). Establishing a standardized accumulation baseline is critical in foliar deposition studies; therefore, sample collection commenced precisely five days following a heavy precipitation event (approximating 19.0 mm of rainfall). Foliage totalling 300 to 400 cm² per species was meticulously harvested from the traffic-facing canopy interface at standardized vertical heights ranging from 0.6 to 2.0 meters above ground level, depending on species architecture. Post-excision, all leaf samples were immediately isolated in pre-labelled, breathable paper bags to prevent moisture condensation and transferred to the laboratory, where they were stored at ambient room temperature prior to gravimetric analysis.
3.3. Leaves were subjected to mechanical agitation for precisely 60 seconds within a glass vessel containing 250 mL of high-purity water to completely dislodge surface particulates. The resulting aqueous suspension was systematically processed via vacuum filtration utilizing nanoporous filter membranes with a calibrated pore diameter of 0.45 µm.
Prior to processing, the filtration membranes were thermally desiccated for 30 minutes at 60 °C and tared in a highly controlled, humidity-stabilized weighing chamber using a micro-analytical balance (XS105DU; Mettler-Toledo International Inc., Switzerland). Following targeted vacuum filtration, the filters were subjected to secondary desiccation and re-weighed. The differential mass established the absolute quantity of captured PM. Concurrently, the total geometric surface area of the washed leaves was quantified utilizing a dedicated leaf area meter (LI-3050A/4; LI-COR Biosciences, USA) paired with SkyeLeaf digital analysis software. Final particulate accumulation metrics were subsequently normalized and expressed as total mass per unit of foliar surface area (g m⁻²).
3.4. Micromorphological Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) To robustly test the hypothesis that PM capture is driven by physical leaf architecture, distinct micro-structural traits were isolated and scanned. Five representative leaves from each sampled species were selected, from which four individual tissue matrices (5 × 5 mm) per lamina were sectioned. These tissues underwent primary chemical fixation in a 2.5% glutaraldehyde medium, followed by controlled rinsing in a 0.1 M phosphate buffer solution.
The fixed matrices were then incrementally dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, terminally stabilized with n-butyl alcohol, and structurally preserved via high-vacuum ion-plating (E-1010; COXEM Co., Ltd., South Korea). High-resolution topography mapping of the epidermal surfaces was executed using an E-1010 Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM; Hitachi, Japan). Leaf surface roughness was qualitatively scaled via an experiential rubric (ranging from 1 = smooth to 5 = high roughness) at 10× optical magnification. Micro-traits were then structurally quantified: localized stomatal density was computed at 300× magnification, whereas the morphological dimensions and densities of epidermal trichomes were measured at 100× magnification.
3.5. Statistical Analyses All recorded empirical datasets were mathematically verified for normal distributions and homoscedasticity prior to executing inferential procedures. Statistical significance amongst the cumulative capture means of the differing plant species was resolved utilizing Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), supplemented by a Least Significant Difference (LSD) metric and Duncan's multiple range test operating at a rigorous significance threshold of p < 0.05.
To categorically stratify the tested species based on their holistic PM accumulation efficiency across the varying contamination zones, advanced K-means clustering algorithms were applied utilizing standardized accumulation matrices. Ultimately, the predictive mechanical influence of individual foliar micro-traits (e.g., leaf size, stomatal length, trichome presence) on gross PM deposition was synthesized through robust multiple regression modeling. Comprehensive statistical computing and dataset aggregation were executed using SPSS Statistics software, version 22.0 (IBM Corp., USA).

4.1. Magnolia grandiflora dominated the biological strata with the highest recorded deposition (4.20 g m⁻²). Zonal stratification brutally exposed the acute mechanical influence of ambient atmospheric loads. Total aggregate capture geometrically scaled with regional anthropogenic contamination: the Industrial Area recorded massive foliar loads ranging precisely from 1.24 to 7.80 g m⁻² (M. grandiflora and P. acerifolia peaked in this extreme strata), heavily outpacing both the Traffic corridor (0.87–4.05 g m⁻²) and the protected University Campus (0.40–2.76 g m⁻²). Vitally, accumulation plasticity was noted even within identical species across differing geographic boundaries (Amorim et al., 2013; Tallis et al., 2011). Taxa such as O. fragrans, L. chinense, and C. japonicum operated at peak biological efficiency within the Traffic and Campus zones, yet abruptly dropped to median volumetric capture within heavily toxic industrial sectors. Distinct genetic efficiency gaps were similarly evident strictly within intra-genus bounds; C. japonicum continuously captured 2.9 to 4.2 times more absolute particulate mass than its close relative, C. camphora, across identical atmospheric exposures (Weerakkody et al., 2018).
4.2. Micromorphological and Architectural Drivers of Particulate Entrapment Rigorous multiple regression modeling successfully isolated specific anatomical drivers of PM sequestration, yielding a virtually flawless predictive mathematical correlation (R² = 0.992) based strictly on leaf micromorphology. These spatial regression arrays definitively proved that particulate retention is directly, positively proportional to localized surface roughness, gross laminar surface area, and epidermal trichome length. Magnolia grandiflora, registering the absolute maximum quantitative capture, natively possessed massive singular leaf areas, expansive guard cell matrices, and dense trichome formations. In stark contrast, the statistically lowest accumulator, C. camphora, exhibited a morphologically slick laminar structure entirely devoid of structural epidermal trichomes.
Counterintuitively, increased stomatal density functioned as a distinctly negative operational variable for total pollutant accumulation (p = 0.041). Ultra-high-density stomatal networks inherently generate robust localized hydrophobicity across the foliar plate, a chemical rejection that drastically reduces the physical contact affinity between the epidermal layer and suspended particulate pollutants (Misson et al., 2005; Enbao et al., 2017). High-efficiency accumulators (M. grandiflora, P. acerifolia, C. japonicum) demonstrated an exact morphological operating threshold, carefully maintaining moderate stomatal densities strictly contained to 32–48 individuals per 300× visual field. These restricted threshold metrics align flawlessly with earlier micro-anatomical frameworks published by (Liu et al. 2012). Furthermore, while ambient particulates heavily load onto adaxial (upper) laminar surfaces, significant microscopic fractionating concurrently occurs via abaxial (lower) stomatal entrapment (Wang and Li, 2006). Collectively, foliage integrating dense, elongated abaxial trichome complexes physically intertwined with restricted stomatal counts represents the absolute optimal biological filter for sustained structural encapsulation (Rasanen et al., 2013; Hwang et al., 2011).
At the macro-morphological scale, towering arbor architectures generally generated higher cumulative purification metrics due to expansive terminal canopy volumes and the passive mechanical induction of localized aerodynamic wind turbulence (McDonald et al., 2007; Lu and Lu, 2015). Nevertheless, gross vertical height alone was not a solitary determinant for success (Ottele et al., 2010; Terzaghi et al., 2013), an operational reality sharply highlighted by the dramatic functional underperformance of the tall C. camphora. Additionally, specialized low-strata shrubs (L. chinense, R. pulchrum) effortlessly sustained heavily elevated deposition loads relative to their reduced total surface area, a specific phenomenon driven by supplementary secondary contamination vectors, prominently localized matrix soil splash (Dzierzanowski et al., 2011). Ultimately, while raw empirical gravimetrics indicated negligible immediate retention variance between deciduous and evergreen blueprints under short atmospheric temporal sampling, evergreen species intrinsically operate with an absolute temporal superiority. Year-round foliar retention actively facilitates continuous, uninterrupted biological monitoring and pollutant physical filtration throughout the entire 12-month meteorological calendar (Radhapriya et al., 2012). Consequently, state-of-the-art urban remediation models must deliberately interface specific, high-roughness biological arbors precisely intertwined with low-lying hairy shrub stratigraphy to maximize multi-level atmospheric scrubbing (Saebo et al., 2012).
5.Conclusion
The systematic investigation of thirteen urban greening plant species across three distinct functional pollution gradients fundamentally advances the applied scientific framework surrounding biological particulate matter (PM) remediation. As atmospheric PM generated from localized industrial and anthropogenic discharges continually drives hazardous respiratory and cardiovascular degradation across urban populations (Bosko et al., 2005; Trnjar et al., 2017), the establishment of mathematically sound, cost-effective vegetative filtration systems has become a critical environmental engineering priority (Tallis et al., 2011; Escobedo et al., 2008). 
The robust empirical gravimetric data generated in this study definitively proves that the biomonitoring and pollutant sequestration efficacy of urban flora is inherently highly plastic and exceptionally species-specific. Total foliar particulate entrapment demonstrated a massive six- to eight-fold overall divergence driven directly by fluctuating localized ambient pollution intensities. Specifically, within severe contamination environments functioning at industrial baselines approximating 488.07 mg m⁻³, taller arbor frameworks such as *Magnolia grandiflora* and *Platanus acerifolia* emerged as the absolute apex biological filters, sustaining unprecedented individual mass accumulations averaging 4.20 g m⁻² and 3.43 g m⁻², respectively. Crucially, the analytical data proved that physically identical genera will operate with drastically divergent efficiencies under the exact same uniform atmospheric stress; across all tested functional zones, *Cinnamomum japonicum* continuously secured 2.9 to 4.2 times more absolute particulate mass than its close biological relative, *Cinnamomum camphora* (which recorded a baseline minimum of 0.99 g m⁻²), definitively invalidating the use of generalized, genus-level urban planting strategies.
More critically, this atmospheric sampling achieved near-perfect predictive mechanical modeling (R² = 0.992) by directly mathematically linking these extreme absolute accumulation variances to highly specific foliar micro-morphologies (Weerakkody et al., 2018). Multi-variable spatial regressions confirmed that optimal biological capture potential is strictly, positively proportional to expanded laminar surface areas, extreme cuticular surface roughness, and highly elongated epidermal trichome matrices physically covering the foliar plate. Conversely, the data revealed that ultra-dense stomatal networks inherently induce powerful localized chemical hydrophobicity across the leaf perimeter. This hydrophobicity operates as a distinctly negative architectural variable, actively and mechanically rejecting the permanent accumulation of suspended ambient inorganic pollutants (Misson et al., 2005; Enbao et al., 2017). 
Ultimately, correctly identifying and structurally quantifying these regulatory micro-anatomical traits provides a rigorous, fundamentally evidence-based engineering metric for modern city planners and environmental architects. Rather than relying on historically generalized aesthetic arbor selections or raw, uncalibrated vegetative volume (1), the targeted, highly deliberate deployment of high-roughness, specifically engineered hairy-leaf evergreens exhibiting strictly moderate stomatal limits will permanently yield the absolute maximum, mathematically verifiable air purification matrix. By strategically prioritizing precise botanical taxa natively possessing these optimized, multi-strata cuticular blueprints, municipal management can successfully engineer continuously active, synergistic biological filtration boundaries capable of drastically reducing airborne atmospheric toxicity and securing long-term regional public health.

1.Background
This study, conducted by (Shi et al. 2017), exists to resolve a critical methodological and empirical gap in how we evaluate the ability of urban plants to filter air pollution.
The primary driver behind this research is the severe public health crisis caused by airborne particulate matter (PM), particularly in rapidly industrializing regions like Beijing. At the time of the study, Beijing's PM2.5 and PM10 concentrations were exceeding secondary regulatory limits by 156% and 54%, respectively, directly correlating with elevated mortality rates from cardiopulmonary diseases and lung cancer. While urban flora was already recognized as biological filters capable of sequestering these airborne pollutants through foliar entrapment, the scientific framework for measuring their efficiency was fundamentally flawed.
Prior to this research, prevailing methodologies quantified foliar PM accumulation almost entirely by weighing the mass of the accumulated dust. Comparatively few investigations evaluated particulate deposition based on absolute numerical density (the actual count of particles).
Most critically, the scientific literature lacked comparative analyses evaluating species-specific number densities of deposited particles stratified into dimensional size fractions—specifically submicron (<1 μm) and fine (1–2.5 μm) particulates. This omission was severe because particle toxicity is inversely proportional to its size; submicron and fine particulates possess heightened bio-reactivity and rapidly penetrate pulmonary barriers. Simply knowing the total mass of dust a leaf captures provides an incomplete picture of its capacity to protect human health.
The Direct Objective The study exists to shift the baseline of phytoremediation evaluation from gravimetric mass to high-resolution dimensional numerical density. By analyzing 14 predominant urban plant species in Beijing utilizing modern scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and X-ray energy dispersive spectrometry (EDS), Shi et al. sought to:
1.	Explicitly isolate and count the microscopic fine and submicron particulates trapped on both the adaxial and abaxial surfaces of leaves.
2.	Determine the elemental origin (natural versus anthropogenic) of these particulates.
3.	Establish a data-driven biological hierarchy, identifying exactly which species are optimized to sequester the specific size fractions of PM that pose the greatest physiological threat to humans.
Ultimately, the research was executed to supply rigid, heavily quantified parameters for urban arboricultural planning, ensuring municipal planting strategies are designed to maximize atmospheric remediation rather than relying on generalized greenery.
2.Study Area
The investigation was situated within the metropolitan matrix of Beijing, China, a geographical zone characterized by critical levels of airborne particulate pollution. Establishing the ambient baseline for the study period, municipal reporting quantified the localized annual mean PM2.5 and PM10 concentrations at 89.5 μg/m³ and 108.1 μg/m³; these metrics exceeded national secondary regulatory thresholds by 156% and 54%, respectively (Beijing Environmental Statement, 2013).
To rigorously evaluate foliar deposition variances modulated by differing urban microclimates, the experimental design necessitated field sampling across two distinct and highly contrasting environmental typologies:
1.	Xizhimen Overpass: Officially designated as a localized traffic pollution monitoring node by the Beijing Municipal Environmental Monitoring Center. This location was explicitly selected to capture foliar accumulation dynamics under the direct, acute influence of heavy vehicular friction and combustion emissions.
2.	Olympic Forest Park: An established urban environmental assessment zone. This site was integrated into the methodology to represent a regulated parkland ecological sector, functioning as a comparative variable exposed primarily to the generalized urban atmospheric background rather than immediate, localized exhaust streams.
The deliberate isolation of these two distinct sampling points allowed researchers to directly quantify how varying intensities of localized anthropogenic stressors alter the particulate sink capacity of urban flora (Shi et al., 2017).
3.Materials and methods
2.1. Plant material and field sampling parameters
To evaluate the particulate matter (PM) monitoring potential across diverse botanical taxa, 14 urban plant species were selected: 11 deciduous trees (Salix matsudana, Ginkgo biloba, Salix babylonica, Fraxinus chinensis, Ulmus pumila, Magnolia denudate, Prunus cerasifera, Amygdalus persica, Populus tomentosa Carr, Sophora japonica, Koelreuteria paniculata), two deciduous shrubs (Sorbaria kirilowii, Amygdalus triloba), and one evergreen shrub (Euonymus japonicus). Plants demonstrating zero pathological or pest-related anomalies were isolated for biological sampling.
To ensure the validity of inter-species comparisons regarding particulate entrapment, temporal and meteorological exposure variables were rigidly standardized. Sampling was conducted at the end of October 2014. Specifically, nine individual plants per species were selected, and exclusively one-year-old foliage was harvested immediately prior to deciduous dehiscence. Biomass acquisition was restricted to periods devoid of rainfall and high wind events to eliminate the confounding variable of transient meteorological wash-off, thereby quantifying the maximum sustained foliar accumulation capability.
2.2. Specimen preparation and electron microscopy quantification
Following field acquisition, foliar samples were sealed in inert plastic chambers and subjected to complete desiccation in a vacuum environment to prevent micro-structural deformation. Symmetrical tissue segments (0.5 cm × 0.5 cm) were excised entirely adjacent to the primary central vein. Distinct replicates were prepared to independently evaluate the adaxial and abaxial epidermal surfaces.
Specimens designated for density quantification were adhered to metallic stubs utilizing double-sided conductive adhesive and stabilized via surface carbon coating. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) was deployed to capture randomized, even epidermal distributions at three defined magnifications to isolate dimensional PM fractions (Ottele et al., 2010): 100× for large particulates (>10 μm); 250× for coarse particulates (2.5–10 μm); and 500× for fine (1–2.5 μm) and submicron (<1 μm) deposits.
For elemental source characterization, uncoated foliar samples were evaluated utilizing an Environmental Scanning Electron Microscope coupled with an X-ray Energy Dispersive Spectrometer (ESEM-EDS; Philips XL30). Analyses were conducted under low vacuum conditions utilizing a 15 KV accelerating voltage. Elemental weight percentages (wt%) were mapped to dynamically differentiate biological background tissues from deposited anthropogenic trace heavy metals.
2.3. Digital spatial analysis and statistical evaluation
SEM micrographs were quantitatively processed utilizing ImageJ spatial analysis software. To eliminate manual observer variance, automated background thresholding algorithms were applied universally across all samples. Aerodynamic particle diameters were mathematically calculated by assuming uniform irregular circular geometries; localized particle contacts registering below a three-pixel spatial threshold were categorically excluded from the comparative biomass dataset (Collins, 2007; Ottele et al., 2010; Sternberg et al., 2010; Zampieri et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2015).
Variance in PM accumulation density and dimensional stratification across the 14 isolated species was evaluated utilizing analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Student’s t-tests (SigmaPlot, Systat Software Inc.). Following this, standardized species-specific size fraction averages were subjected to K-mean multivariate spatial clustering (SPSS) to rigidly classify the taxa into high, intermediate, and low accumulation efficiency tiers, establishing their comparative efficacy as established urban bio-monitors.
4.Result and Discussion
4.1. Elemental characterization and validation of anthropogenic monitoring
Compositional analysis of foliar-deposited particulates resolved an aggregation of natural and anthropogenic source elements, validating the comparative biological monitoring capability of the selected species (Marszalek, 2008; Hermosin et al., 2004). Entrapped elemental matrices consisted predominantly of C (50.18%) and O (31.74%), representing intrinsic biological cellular tissues. While background terrestrial markers (Si, Ca, Na, Mg, K) were consistently isolated, the robust spatial retention of Pb, Fe, S, and Cl, alongside multiple transition metals (Ni, Cu, Zn, Pd, Cd), verified external anthropogenic entrapment. The persistent detection of Pb, Fe, S, and Cl serves as a physical analog for surrounding diesel combustion, vehicular friction, and regional coal emission data (Searle, 2001; Ottele et al., 2010; Song et al., 2015), effectively establishing the baseline to verify these morphological variants as operational metallic bio-monitors.
4.2. Comparative spatial density and dimensional stratification
Inter-species variance regarding absolute particulate accumulation was statistically significant (P < 0.001), registering a ~1.9-fold differential between the maximum and minimum botanical sink capacities. The aggregate mean particulate count across all 14 evaluated biomonitors was established at 50,961.5 particles/mm².
Dimensional fractional analysis confirmed a profound deposition distribution heavily skewed toward highly reactive nanoparticles: submicron (<1 μm) and fine (1–2.5 μm) particulates constituted 52.9% and 25.0% of the total recorded deposition, respectively. Coarse (2.5–10 μm) and large (>10 μm) variants comprised strictly 16.2% and 5.9%. When comparing specific botanical taxa, Euonymus japonicus recorded the absolute maximum submicron entrapment capabilities, with submicron particles representing 62.95% of its total PM load at the combustion-heavy Overpass location and 54.76% at the Park location. In direct functional contrast, Populus tomentosa Carr and Ulmus pumila recorded the lowest submicron accumulation capacities (38.92% for U. pumila in the Park), confirming severe morphologically induced bias in size-specific atmospheric filtration.
4.3. Adaxial/abaxial deposition dynamics
Particulate accumulation efficiencies exhibited extreme spatial heterogeneity across leaf planes. Densities recorded on adaxial surfaces proved 1.6 to 7.5 times greater than paired abaxial cross-sections. Despite this differential volume, abaxial deposition strictly accounted for 24% of the overarching localized particulate count. While previous gravimetric analyses determined abaxial mass captures to represent approximately 17% of total load (Wang et al., 2006), the 24% numerical prevalence identified by Shi et al. functionally dictates that abaxial-trapped loads are almost exclusively populated by high-density submicron fractions. The massive gross capture discrepancy favoring the adaxial surface correlates mathematically to elevated adaxial surface roughness and perpendicular exposure to turbulent transfer impacts (Ottele et al., 2010).
4.4. Classification of taxonomic biomonitoring potential
To physically synthesize inter-species biomonitoring efficacy, taxa were subjected to K-mean spatial clustering based directly upon standardized dimensional accumulation metrics. The data isolated maximum overall density disparities of 35,790.2 particles/mm² and 30,093.3 particles/mm² at the Overpass and Park environments, respectively.
The high-efficiency biomonitoring cluster (Cluster 1) localized to the severe-pollution Overpass site was comprised of Salix matsudana, E. japonicus, Magnolia denudate, Fraxinus chinensis, Sophora japonica, and Ginkgo biloba. This group yielded a dominant aggregated mean capture density of 66,908.5 particles/mm². Within the baseline Park ecosystem, E. japonicus and S. matsudana maintained comparable dominant retention rates (mean density: 62,318.7 particles/mm²). In inverse comparison, a functionally inferior biocluster—comprising U. pumila, Sorbaria kirilowii, Amygdalus triloba, and P. tomentosa Carr—failed to exceed a mean operational threshold of 36,895.3 particles/mm².
When optimizing specifically for severe cardiopulmonary hazard mitigation (isolation of submicron and fine PM), S. matsudana, E. japonicus, M. denudate, S. japonica, F. chinensis, and G. biloba fundamentally outperformed all remaining 8 taxonomic variants. Within this defined subclass, S. matsudana consistently secured highly stabilized submicron entrapment ratios across both the extreme (53.17%) and baseline (54.57%) atmospheric environments. Comparative retention efficacy was physically linked to individual botanical micromorphology—specifically the absolute density of epidermal heaves, spatial grooves, and cuticular gland secretions (Wang et al., 2006). Crucially, the mathematical outperformance of the evergreen shrub E. japonicus across both experimental sites empirically verifies that specific low-canopy biomonitoring configurations maintain accumulation potentials mathematically equivalent, or actively superior, to highly-developed arboreal topologies (Saebo et al., 2012).
5.Conclusion
The quantitative comparative evaluation of 14 established urban plant species verifies significant inter-taxa variances in particulate accumulation, establishing explicit functional baselines for biomonitoring selection within hyper-polluted atmospheric zones (Beijing Environmental Statement, 2013). Compositional validation confirmed that foliar matrices operate as rigid physical sinks capable of isolating localized anthropogenic terrestrial pollution, traced physically via the spatial retention of localized heavy metal aggregates including Fe, S, Cl, and Pb.
The entrapped atmospheric load is radically skewed toward highly reactive micro-geometries. Across all measured species, the localized mean accumulation density registered at 50,961.5 particles/mm². Within this metric, submicron (<1 μm) and fine (1–2.5 μm) dimensions mathematically constituted exactly 52.9% and 25.0% of the aggregate load, respectively. Analysis of physical deposition dynamics confirmed that while the adaxial plane overwhelmingly captures the bulk volume, the corresponding abaxial surface possesses a critical numerical sink capacity, accounting for strictly 24% of the total entrapped particle count. This specific prevalence of abaxial numerical capture directly contrasts with prior gravimetric mass models determining abaxial retention at only 17% (Wang et al., 2006), verifying that abaxial loads are heavily submicron in definition. Consequently, evaluating abaxial particle density is mandatory for developing accurate cross-taxa evaluations of future urban bio-monitors (Ottele et al., 2010).

From a taxonomic comparative hierarchy, *Salix matsudana, Euonymus japonicus, Magnolia denudate, Sophora japonica, Amygdalus persica,* and *Salix babylonica* function as the maximum-efficiency botanical models for overall PM retention. When calibrating selection criteria specifically for the sequestration of submicron and fine respiratory hazards, the quantitative data isolates *S. matsudana, E. japonicus, M. denudate, S. japonica, Fraxinus chinensis,* and *Ginkgo biloba* as the superior biological configurations. Notably, the extreme submicron accumulation density explicitly documented within the evergreen shrub *E. japonicus* validates existing hypotheses (Saebo et al., 2012) specifying that lower-canopy structural shrubs possess persistent biomonitoring capacities mathematically equivalent to, or operating beyond, standard broadleaf arboreal canopies. Integrating these specific size-fractionated density variances establishes the definitive empirical criteria necessary to select highly specific, species-dependent flora matrices optimized to monitor localized aerodynamic pollution.

1.Background
Deteriorating air quality in metropolitan environments remains a primary public health concern, largely driven by concentrated anthropogenic emissions from traffic and industry. Among the various monitored atmospheric pollutants, particulate matter (PMx) presents an acute localized hazard. This suspended material consists of a complex matrix of solid and liquid substances, frequently containing heavy metals, unburned black carbon, and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons. The potential for physiological harm increases as the aerodynamic particle size decreases. Fractions falling below 2.5 micrometers readily bypass primary respiratory defences to penetrate deep into pulmonary tissues, initiating severe respiratory and cardiovascular conditions. The epidemiological impact resulting from these exposures is severe; environmental assessments link atmospheric degradation to approximately 62,000 annual mortalities in Italy alone (European Environment Agency, 2023).
Urban green spaces offer an effective, passive biological mechanism to mitigate these concentrations. Shrub and tree canopies deployed along high-traffic corridors act as physical barriers, capturing suspended PMx directly onto vegetative surfaces. Extensive studies quantify this environmental service across varying geographic scales. For example, modeling of natural urban green infrastructure in Great Britain demonstrated the removal of 28,700 tonnes of combined pollutants throughout 2015, averting hundreds of adverse hospital admissions (Jones et al., 2019). Similarly, assessments in the United States estimate that a single hectare of urban tree cover can isolate approximately 67 kilograms of atmospheric pollution annually (Nowak et al., 2014).
Despite these documented benefits, the capacity to sequester particulate matter is not uniform across plant species. Accumulation efficiency is heavily dictated by a species' specific morphological and structural leaf traits. Physical parameters, such as total leaf area, structural geometry, and surface roughness, determine how effectively particles adhere to the foliage. Recent investigations by Baesso Moura et al. (2024) tested these variable relationships directly across thirty different plant species. Their multiple linear regression modeling identified a negative correlation between overall PMx accumulation and both specific leaf area (SLA) and absolute leaf area (LA). Conversely, geometric leaf roundness showed a significant positive correlation with entrapment efficiency. Micro-structural elements are equally critical to this process; distinct layers of epicuticular wax serve to permanently anchor deposited particulates against potential wind and rain resuspension.
Because this sequestration capacity varies so widely between taxa, standardizing the underlying quantification methodology is essential before attempting to evaluate different plants. Formally assessing which species function as the most effective bio-monitors requires precise, validated laboratory techniques. Researchers typically rely on two primary assessment protocols: optical coverage estimation using light microscopy and size-fractionated gravimetric filtration. Baesso Moura et al. (2024) evaluated the direct correspondence between these discrete techniques, reporting a strong statistical correlation (R² = 0.72) that confirms both approaches yield reliable foliar accumulation data.
Applying this validated filtration method successfully isolated the most capable botanical accumulators. Five specific taxa—Euonymus japonicus, Ligustrum lucidum, Alnus glutinosa, Rubus ulmifolius, and Laurus nobilis—demonstrated exceptional accumulation profiles, with total PMx loads routinely exceeding 50 µg cm⁻². When isolating the most hazardous fine fractions measuring between 2.0 and 2.5 µm, E. japonicus and L. lucidum proved highly effective, successfully capturing absolute concentrations greater than 10 µg cm⁻² (Baesso Moura et al., 2024). Ultimately, confirming this methodological reliability provides the necessary empirical foundation for accurately reviewing and comparing the true monitoring potential of established plant bio-monitors across the literature.

The baseline for evaluating these plant bio-monitors was established in the province of Lucca (Tuscany, Italy), a region subjected to chronically high particulate matter (PMx) concentrations. The sampling network spanned multiple distinct municipal zones, balanced against seasonal, climatic, and logistical parameters to accurately represent the region's overall biodiversity.

Sampling strictly targeted leaves fully exposed to the surrounding exterior air to capture a true representation of maximum PMx impaction. Immediately following collection, all organic samples were secured directly in paper bags for stable transport to the laboratory for subsequent imaging and gravimetric filtration analyses.
3.Materials and methods
3.1. Optical Assessment via Light Microscopy Initial determinations of foliar particulate coverage relied on standardized visual screening. From each collected species, researchers isolated two distinct 1 cm² square sections per leaf. These biological sections underwent direct adaxial observation under 10× optical magnification. The resulting micro-captures were imported directly into ImageJ software (imagej.net/ij/; Schneider et al., 2012) to accurately quantify the total percentage of the foliar surface obscured by PMx. Spatial calibration within the software interface required aligning the internal measurement line tool against a known physical scale bar, manually inputting a set distance of 0.5, and applying this configuration globally across all analyzed frames.
Particle extraction operations utilized the ImageJ native Particle Analysis Grid plugin, setting the grid type to "line" and configuring the exact area per point to 100,000 µm². Analysts magnified randomized square fields of view to 200% and sequentially isolated visible particulate matter utilizing both the software’s Magic Wand and Ellipse tools. Every individually selected PMx instance was appended to the Region of Interest (ROI) Manager. Activating the measurement protocol generated aggregate dimensional data within the Results window, which was subsequently exported for spreadsheet analysis. Applying this methodology dictated the primary evaluation baseline; only plant species exhibiting a strict minimum threshold of ≥1% total foliar PMx coverage advanced to the mechanical filtration phase.
3.2. Gravimetric Filtration Protocol Extracting absolute solid particulate concentrations required executing an established serial filtration methodology (Mori et al., 2018). Following exact baseline leaf area (LA) measurements, intact biological samples were submerged in 150 mL of deionized water and mechanically agitated for exactly 60 seconds to detach surface matter. A vacuum pump connected to a 47 mm glass filter funnel drove the resulting aqueous suspension through three specific fractionating membranes.
The filtration sequence targeted discrete aerodynamic boundaries using three distinct barriers: initially passing fluid through a Sartorius FT-3-104-055 filter (isolating PMx ≤ 10 µm), subsequently through a Sartorius FT-3-354-110 filter (isolating PMx ≤ 2.5 µm), and finally forcing the remaining liquid through a Sartorius 11807-47-N PTFE membrane (isolating ultrafine PMx ≤ 0.2 µm). Prior to physical usage, every membrane underwent a stringent conditioning cycle: forced thermal drying at 60 °C for 30 minutes, followed immediately by a 60-minute environmental stabilization phase inside a chamber maintaining a constant 50% relative humidity. Only after this stabilization were the initial tare weights documented. Upon completing fluid filtration, researchers precisely replicated this exact thermal and humidity conditioning protocol before securing the final loaded weights. Final species concentrations for every fractional size were recorded consistently as µg cm⁻².
3.3. Morphological Leaf Trait Analysis Correlating localized particulate entrapment with vegetative architecture demanded comprehensive structural mapping. Three uniform leaves extracted from every sample group were photographed against extreme high-contrast white backgrounds. Processing these high-resolution visual captures utilizing ImageJ generated core structural metrics, defined as total leaf surface area (LA, in cm²), leaf dissection index (LDI, calculated geometrically as leaf perimeter / √leaf area), and structural leaf roundness (computed utilizing the formula: (4 × leaf area) / [π × major axis]). Specific leaf area (SLA, expressed as m² kg⁻¹) determinations required destructive physical sampling. The methodology isolated five separate 8 mm diameter discs from individual leaves across all species, subsequently drying the extracted organic material until realizing a constant absolute weight. This stabilized dry matter baseline informed the calculation of total leaf mass per area (LMA) to finalize SLA variables.
3.4. Statistical Methodologies Verifying fundamental correlation accuracy between the discrete visual and gravimetric analytical methods relied on linear regression testing incorporating aggregate data across all 30 evaluated species. Validating these exact methodological approaches necessitated utilizing pooled foliar samples representing each species. This bulk grouping intentionally isolated raw physical measurement divergence by suppressing background inter-species variability.
To define the explicit bio-physical drivers behind particulate accumulation, researchers applied multiple linear regression (MLR) modeling against specific foliar traits representing eight targeted species. These eight taxa were isolated specifically because their collection occurred concurrently on a singular date within a shared municipal boundary (UT zone). This rigid spatial and temporal alignment guaranteed that all analyzed samples experienced identical exposure to background aerosolized pollution matrices—an absolute environmental requirement for maintaining viable comparative statistics (Muhammad et al., 2019). The complete analytical array, including all multi-variable plotting and probability testing, was generated processing datasets through Origin (Pro) software, version 2023b (Origin Lab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA).

1.Background
Airborne particulate matter represents a severe environmental and public health crisis in rapidly expanding metropolitan regions across the globe (Li et al., 2021; Nations, 2018; Xu et al., 2020). Fine and ultrafine suspended particles, particularly those under 10 micrometres in aerodynamic diameter, account for over half of all total suspended particulates (WHO, 2021). These particles are easily inhaled and bypass primary biological filtration systems to penetrate deep into the respiratory tract (Schwarze et al., 2006; Zhang et al., 2021), a mechanism that strongly correlates with an increased incidence of cardiovascular and pulmonary pathologies (Chen et al., 2022; Maher et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2020). To mitigate these localized atmospheric threats, environmental management strategies increasingly rely on urban green infrastructure as a primary remediation tool. Tree canopies act as passive aerodynamic filters, providing complex foliar surfaces that intercept airborne contaminants and facilitate continuous dry deposition. This naturally occurring filtration process significantly diminishes the ambient concentration of hazardous particles, thereby reducing human exposure in densely populated landscapes (Abhijith & Kumar, 2020; Janhäll, 2015; Kumar et al., 2025; Miao et al., 2023; Sgrigna et al., 2020).
While the baseline capability of vegetation to sequester airborne pollutants is well established, the fundamental mechanisms governing the specific size distribution and chemical composition of the deposited particles remain a subject of considerable scientific debate. The existing literature reveals a distinct dichotomy regarding the primary drivers of this accumulation. A substantial body of research posits that particulate retention is primarily a function of inter-specific biological traits. This perspective suggests that variations in cuticular wax chemistry, stomatal architecture, overall canopy structure, and surface micro-roughness grant different plant taxa highly selective adsorption capabilities (Chen et al., 2024a; Gaglio et al., 2022; He et al., 2020; Sgrigna et al., 2020; Steinparzer et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2021). As a result, different tree species exhibit distinct variations in dry deposition rates and capture capabilities for various particle sizes and chemical compositions, acting somewhat independently of the surrounding atmospheric mixture (Chen et al., 2017; Shahrukh et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Xue et al., 2025).
Conversely, a parallel line of evidence asserts the overriding influence of localized emission sources. Studies conducted near aggressive pollution vectors, such as industrial zones or heavy traffic corridors, indicate that a highly concentrated ambient particulate matrix profoundly shapes the deposition profile (Baldacchini et al., 2019; Faber et al., 2015; Miller-Schulze et al., 2015; Popek et al., 2015, 2022; Wei et al., 2021). Under these high-pressure conditions, foliage is reduced to a passive environmental receptacle that simply mirrors the local chemical signature. This effectively renders species-specific anatomical adaptations irrelevant, as the pollution source imprints its specific chemical markers and size fractions directly onto the leaf surfaces.
This theoretical divergence exposes a critical limitation in contemporary urban forestry planning, as the interaction between biological capture efficiency and environmental source heterogeneity is demonstrably context-dependent. Historically, the scientific community has relied on qualitative observations to determine whether the ambient environment or the plant anatomy dominates the depositional process. A fundamental uncertainty has persisted regarding the precise thresholds at which ambient source heterogeneity suppresses biological traits, or conversely, when environmental homogeneity permits species-specific morphology to govern retention. Advancing green infrastructure from generalized aesthetic landscaping to precision environmental engineering requires transitioning beyond these qualitative assumptions. There is a distinct necessity for a reproducible, quantitative analytical framework capable of indexing the shift between source-dominant and species-dominant regimes, which is vital for optimizing targeted pollutant removal across diverse urban micro-environments.
2.Study Area
To effectively investigate the contextual boundaries between biological selective adsorption and environmental source dominance, field experiments require a highly stratified urban landscape with clearly defined emission zones. The research isolates its study area within the metropolitan expanse of Shanghai, China. Situated in the Yangtze River Delta, this megacity represents a high-density urban environment characterized by complex, overlapping emission vectors. Furthermore, the region relies heavily on specific adaptive vegetation for municipal greening, providing an ideal real-world testing ground for evaluating widespread urban tree species. The local deployment of the broadleaf Cinnamomum camphora and the coniferous Sabina chinensis makes them highly representative subjects for assessing particulate matter retention across the city.
The spatial experimental design strategically divided the study area into three distinct sampling locations, selected specifically for the contrasting heterogeneity of their localized atmospheric emissions. This tri-site approach allowed the researchers to establish controlled environmental groups to test how varying degrees of pollution similarity influence foliar accumulation. The first location, designated as the T-C-P site, operates under a highly mixed, co-dominant pollution profile. It sits just 0.5 kilometres from a major expressway, 1 kilometre from a coal-fired power plant, and 3 kilometres from a designated chemical industrial zone. Consequently, the atmospheric matrix here is a dense blend of vehicular elemental and organic carbon, combined with industrial sulphates, nitrates, fly ash, and heavy metals.
The second location, identified as the T-P site, was selected to provide a relatively homogeneous comparison to the first. Located 0.5 kilometres from the expressway and 4 kilometres from the power plant, it shares the same primary combustion aerosol profile as the T-C-P site but completely lacks the immediate chemical industry influence. By comparing these two combustion-heavy zones, researchers could observe exactly how plant species behave when environmental variables and local atmospheric chemistry are closely aligned.
In sharp contrast to the industrial zones, the third location serves as the high-heterogeneity control. Designated as the T-D site, this area is defined entirely by vehicular traffic and road dust. It is characterized by heavy private vehicle circulation with absolutely no industrial or power plant emission sources within a 5-kilometer radius, creating an environment completely dominated by fine, traffic-derived particulates.
To ensure that the particulate matter accumulating on the foliage genuinely reflected these specific nearby sources without outside interference, the physical location data was strictly correlated with local meteorological conditions. Information retrieved from local meteorological stations confirmed that the entire seven-day exposure window occurred during a period of high atmospheric stability. The region experienced a total absence of precipitation, preventing any premature rain wash-off of the deposited particles. Furthermore, wind speeds consistently remained below five meters per second, predominantly blowing from the north and northeast. This sustained, low-velocity directional wind was a critical feature of the study area, as it ensured that emissions from the targeted highways and industrial zones were reliably and continuously carried directly over the sampled tree canopies.
3.Materials and Method
To accurately isolate the accumulation rates of different plant species, strict baseline controls were established prior to environmental exposure. Three healthy specimens, ranging from five to ten years of age, were randomly selected for both Cinnamomum camphora and Sabina chinensis at each of the three monitoring sites. At an approximate canopy height of 3 meters, researchers randomly designated between 200 and 600 mature leaves, ensuring even distribution across the east, south, west, and north facing branches.
To create a zero-accumulation baseline, all selected foliage was washed three times with deionized water to completely detach any pre-existing particulate matter. The cleaned leaves were intentionally left intact on their respective branches so normal physiological and transportational activities could continue uninterrupted. Each leaf was physically tagged to ensure exact traceability. The foliage was then subjected to the ambient urban atmosphere for exactly seven days. During this natural exposure window, localized suspended particulate matter was actively tracked using a Single Particle Aerosol Mass Spectrometer (SPAMS 0515, Hexin Analytical Instrument Co., Ltd., Guangzhou). On the morning following the seventh day, the exposed leaves were harvested, sealed inside sterile aluminium foil bags, and immediately transported to the laboratory under controlled conditions.
Evaluating the deposited particles required separating them from the leaf tissue without causing mechanical damage that might introduce biological debris into the chemical analysis. This was achieved using a custom Particulate Matter Resuspension Chamber (PMRC). The PMRC is a 0.69 cubic-meter stainless-steel enclosure equipped with glove handling ports, an internal circulation fan, and dedicated injection ports for 99.9% high-purity nitrogen carrier gas (Chen et al., 2024b; Lyu et al., 2023).
Processing followed a highly regulated five-step procedure. Initially, the internal chamber was decontaminated by washing the walls with deionized water and purging the space with nitrogen gas until the ambient background particulate concentration dropped below 5 µg/m³. This baseline purity was strictly verified using a GRIMM 11-R particle size spectrometer. Leaf samples were then mounted inside the chamber under active fan circulation operating at 1 m/s. Controlled internal airflow gently dislodged the surface-deposited particulate matter, dispersing it homogeneously throughout the chamber. The resuspended particulates were pneumatically drawn through an 8-millimeter stainless steel conduit directly into the SPAMS inlet, maintaining a highly regulated transfer flow of 80 mL/min via a 100 µm critical orifice. Upon completion of the analysis, a final nitrogen purge reset the chamber environment for the subsequent sample batch.
Chemical and physical characterization was executed simultaneously utilizing the SPAMS unit. As the resuspended particles entered the instrument, they were accelerated through an aerodynamic lens assembly where their specific velocities correlated directly to their physical diameters. Exact aerodynamic sizing was accomplished as the particles passed through dual continuous-diode neodymium-doped yttrium aluminium garnet (Nd:YAG) lasers operating at a 532 nm wavelength.
Following sizing, the particles underwent intense ionization via a pulsed 266 nm Nd:YAG laser. This laser delivered 0.6 mJ of pulse energy at a massive power density approximating 1.06 × 10⁸ W/cm². The resulting explosive fragmentation generated both positive and negative ions. These fragments were immediately intercepted by a bipolar time-of-flight mass spectrometer, generating detailed, dual-polarity spectral data for every individual particle (Hu et al., 2021; Peng et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2017).
Raw mass spectral data was processed using a MATLAB-based SPAMS Data Analysis Toolkit. To eliminate instrumental background noise and ensure data integrity, hard minimum thresholds were established: peak heights below 10 units and peak areas below 20 units were discarded from the dataset (Giorio et al., 2015).
The filtered ion signatures were fed into an ART-2a neural network algorithm to automate the classification of the ionized particles. The machine learning parameters were set with a vigilance factor of 0.75, a learning rate of 0.05, and a cap of 20 iterations. The resulting outputs were manually verified and grouped into ten distinct chemical classes (Chen et al., 2024b).
To transition from raw chemical identification to a comparative ecological assessment, hierarchical clustering was applied to the data utilizing average-linkage distances derived from Bray-Curtis dissimilarities. This clustering approach enabled the calculation of a novel Context Dominance Index (CDI), designed to quantify exactly which variable controlled the particle deposition. The CDI is mathematically defined as the ratio between the average-linkage distance among different pollution environments (D_env) and the distance among different tree species within the same environment (D_sp). A CDI value exceeding 1.0 mathematically proves a source-dominant regime where local pollution overrides plant traits, while a value below 1.0 confirms a species-dominant regime where plant morphology dictates chemical retention.
4.Result and Discussion
Cluster analyses of ambient and foliar-deposited particulate matter reveal that particle size distributions respond acutely to the heterogeneity of local pollution sources. Across all sampled environments, particles within the 0.5 to 1.0 µm range constituted the baseline majority. However, at the traffic-dust (T-D) dominated site, this size fraction overwhelmingly exceeded 80% of the total deposited particulate matter for both Cinnamomum camphora and Sabina chinensis. This pronounced physical convergence caused samples from both tree species to cluster together identically, independent of their biological traits. The ambient environment at the T-D site is heavily saturated with traffic-derived fine particles (PM1), which persist in suspension for extended periods and utilize their expansive specific surface area to readily adhere to foliage via localized airflow and water vapor condensation (Ahmad et al., 2023; Benabed & Boulbair, 2022; Eftekhari et al., 2024; Manigrasso et al., 2019). Under these highly heterogeneous source conditions, foliage is relegated to a passive receptor role, exclusively capturing the locally dominant fine vehicular emissions (Lee et al., 2017; Lintusaari et al., 2023; Lonati & Giugliano, 2006). This source-overriding mechanism is quantitatively validated by the calculated Context Dominance Index (CDI). When evaluating size distributions between the highly contrasting T-D and T-P/T-C-P environments, the environmental Bray-Curtis distance reached 0.33 against an inter-species distance of only 0.13. The resulting CDI of 2.49 mathematically confirms that ambient environmental variance entirely suppresses species-specific particulate capture traits.
Shifting the analysis to the comparatively homogeneous combustion-driven environments (the T-P and T-C-P sites) exposes a distinct pivot toward biological selectivity. At these locations, the proportion of 0.5 to 1.0 µm particles dropped to a range of 45% to 62%, reflecting ambient profiles weighted toward larger particulates characteristic of industrial emission controls (Saarnio et al., 2014; Sgrigna et al., 2020; Sui et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2024). As local source variations weakened—reflected by a narrow environmental distance of 0.12 and a CDI dropping to 0.71—plant morphological differences became the primary regulators of deposition. The rough, scale-like needle structure of S. chinensis provided enhanced physical interception sites, driving a preferential accumulation of coarse particles. In contrast, the smooth cuticular interface of C. camphora facilitated superior capture of ultrafine particles via Brownian diffusion (Chen et al., 2024a; Ho et al., 2012; Hwang et al., 2011; Spielman-Sun et al., 2020; Xue et al., 2025). This physical sorting is likely compounded by localized accumulation feedbacks. Initial deposition of coarse material on S. chinensis may trigger a blocking effect, physically shielding the leaf surface from subsequent fine particle impingement (Chen et al., 2022; Terzaghi et al., 2013). Concurrently, these same coarse deposits may initiate microenvironment remodeling, altering the surface topography to generate new micro-sites that paradoxically enhance the localized entrapment of smaller diffusional particles (Burkhardt & Hunsche, 2013; Fernández et al., 2014; Tomson et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2015a, 2015b).
The chemical speciation of the captured particulates mirrors this size-based dichotomy, providing robust secondary evidence for context-dependent deposition. At the traffic-isolated T-D site, single-particle aerosol mass spectrometry detected severe enrichment of elemental carbon (EC), organic carbon (OC), and levoglucosan (LEV) markers on the leaves of both plant species. This intense carbonaceous signature, coupled with a distinct deficit in crustal elements, perfectly aligns with known vehicular exhaust profiles, further establishing that heavy transport emissions overwhelm biological filtration limits (Lee et al., 2017; Tial et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2017). This chemical convergence across the two species generated an environmental distance of 0.33 against a species distance of 0.17, yielding a composition-based CDI of 1.94. A chemical CDI well above 1.0 strictly confirms a source-dominant regime where both broadleaf and coniferous structures act merely as passive recorders of local traffic chemistry.
When comparing the co-dominant industrial and power plant sites (T-P and T-C-P), the chemical signatures shifted heavily toward crustal components (Al⁺, Ca⁺, Fe⁺, Ti⁺) and heavy metals (Pb⁺), directly reflecting the regional metallurgical and coal-fired emissions (Turhan et al., 2020; Wei et al., 2021). Because the ambient chemical background between these two sites was relatively uniform (generating an environmental distance of just 0.07), inter-species chemical selectivity re-emerged as the governing dynamic, pulling the CDI down to an indicator of 0.43. Under these species-dominant conditions, the coniferous S. chinensis exhibited a massive affinity for heavy metals, adsorbing 2.3 times the HM concentrations captured by its broadleaf counterpart. This localized heavy metal enrichment is driven by the inherently high surface free energy and viscosity characteristic of the species' rough needles (Lyu et al., 2023; Schreck et al., 2012). Conversely, C. camphora exhibited a specialized retention capacity for levoglucosan, capturing 2.0 times the LEV concentrations observed on the pines, an isolation mechanism heavily regulated by the specific interactions between the organic compounds and the plant's hydrophobic waxy cuticle (Spielman-Sun et al., 2020).
These dual physical and chemical datasets resolve a fundamental uncertainty in urban phytoremediation by proving that particulate retention is neither exclusively source-driven nor exclusively biology-driven, but rather dynamically context-dependent. The Context Dominance Index successfully quantifies exactly when this shift occurs. In high-heterogeneity, source-dominant environments (CDI > 1), urban design cannot rely on specialized tree filters; mitigation must prioritize strict emission controls at the source alongside the bulk deployment of high-leaf-area, pollution-tolerant vegetation. However, in homogeneous emission zones (CDI < 1), urban planners can deploy green infrastructure as highly targeted filtration arrays. Identifying that S. chinensis acts as an aggressive sink for coarse, metal-rich industrial fallout while C. camphora excels at trapping ultrafine, organic-rich particulates provides a concrete, data-backed blueprint for precision-engineered urban greening.
5.Conclusion
Resolving a persistent debate within urban phytoremediation, the evidence confirms that particulate matter accumulation on foliage is a highly dynamic process rather than a static biological function. The data proves that neither environmental pollution profiles nor distinct plant traits hold absolute control over particle capture. Instead, the governing mechanism shifts based entirely on the spatial variance of local emissions.
The introduction of a quantitative metric, the Context Dominance Index, finally moves this field past qualitative guesswork. When local environments feature severe emission heterogeneity—such as aggressive traffic corridors—the sheer density of the ambient chemical signature overrides any inherent biological selectivity. Under these high-pressure conditions, tree leaves act as nothing more than passive environmental sinks. Consequently, mitigation efforts in these zones cannot rely on specialized biological filtration; they require strict emission controls paired with the mass planting of resilient, large-canopy species.
Conversely, within relatively uniform pollution matrices, inter-specific physiological differences step up as the primary regulators. In these biological-dominant regimes, city planners can actually exploit specific plant morphologies to target specific pollutants. For example, coniferous needles can be deployed to intercept coarse, heavy-metal fallout, while smooth broadleaves can be used to sequester ultrafine carbonaceous compounds.
While this framework marks a major step forward for environmental engineering, the current scope primarily looks at the composition of easily resuspended particles rather than total dry deposition mass. To scale these insights from individual leaf interactions up to municipal-level modeling, future research needs to measure absolute mass accumulation across a much wider variety of canopy structures and plant types. By mathematically defining the relationship between ambient pollution and biological filtration, this research lays down a concrete, data-backed blueprint for executing the "right tree, right place" strategy. This ensures that urban green infrastructure is systematically optimized to maximize public health interventions and broad-scale ecological air quality benefits.

A green, fast protocol to estimate the accumulation of airborne anthropogenic microfibers in Pittosporum tobira in urban areas: effects of season and rainfall. Anna Gaglione, Angelo Granata, Fiore Capozzi, Antonio Rallo, Simonetta Giordano, Maria Cristina Sorrentino and Valeria Spagnuolo
1.Background
Synthetic polymers have completely taken over global manufacturing. Because they are cheap, waterproof, and highly durable, plastics are used in everything from everyday packaging to heavy construction materials. In 2021 alone, global plastic production reached roughly 390 million tons, with the vast majority derived directly from fossil fuels. The fundamental issue with this explosive production is that these materials take anywhere from decades to millennia to naturally degrade. Instead of safely disappearing, plastic waste slowly breaks down into increasingly smaller fragments, generating massive quantities of microplastics (particles ranging from 1 micrometre to 5 millimetres) and even smaller nanoplastics.
While the accumulation of plastic debris in oceans, freshwater systems, and soil has been extensively documented, atmospheric plastic pollution has flown largely under the radar until very recently. Airborne microplastics are widely underestimated in current environmental assessments, even though they have been discovered settling in some of the most remote and inhospitable locations on the planet. Across both indoor and outdoor urban environments, synthetic microfibers represent the most prevalent type of airborne plastic. These fibrous pollutants are constantly ejected into the atmosphere from daily human activities, including the friction of synthetic clothing, waste incineration, degrading building materials, and road traffic—the latter of which alone is estimated to generate 7 million kilograms of emissions every year. Weather patterns and wind then heavily dictate how these fibers are transported and deposited across cities.
Tracking how these microfibers disperse is notoriously difficult and resource-intensive. Conventional air quality monitoring relies on mechanical passive samplers, which are expensive to deploy at scale and require specific technical expertise to operate correctly. To bypass these limitations, environmental scientists have started looking toward Phyto monitoring. Plant foliage naturally intercepts and traps airborne particulate matter due to its high surface-to-mass ratio, making urban trees an excellent, cost-effective alternative to mechanical sensors. However, the scientific community still lacks a reliable, standardized, and fast protocol for using plant leaves specifically to monitor atmospheric synthetic microfibers.
This specific paper exists to address that exact methodological gap. Building on previous experimental work that identified the common evergreen shrub Pittosporum tobira as an effective biological trap for airborne fibres, the authors set out to validate a rapid, low-cost extraction technique that simply utilizes adhesive tape to strip fibres directly from the leaf cuticle. The primary objective of the study is to determine if P. tobira leaves are sensitive enough to accurately map varying intensities of microfiber pollution across different urban zones, including industrial areas, city centres, and green parks. Furthermore, the research specifically aims to uncover how seasonal weather shifts—particularly the physical washing effect of heavy pouring rain—alter the plant's ability to retain these microscopic pollutants over time.
2.Study Area
To properly evaluate the dispersion and accumulation of airborne anthropogenic microfibers across varying environmental pressures, the field investigation was conducted within the municipality of Naples, Italy. The sampling perimeter encompassed a geographic boundary approximately twelve kilometres in diameter, successfully capturing a densely populated and highly heterogeneous metropolitan landscape. Within this defined boundary, the spatial design incorporated six specific monitoring locations deliberately chosen to reflect contrasting land-use profiles. These sites were systematically stratified into heavy industrial zones, high-traffic urban centres, and municipal green parks, ensuring that the monitoring network was subjected to a wide spectrum of localized emission sources and anthropogenic activities.
The region's meteorological profile is defined by distinct seasonal shifts that fundamentally influence the dynamics of atmospheric pollutant deposition. The local climate exhibits a pronounced dry season during the summer, which contrasts sharply with a wet winter period characterized by intense, concentrated precipitation events. Meteorological records captured during the study indicated that typical winter downpours could easily exceed thirty millimetres of rain within a twenty-four-hour window. Concurrently, overall wind velocities remained relatively low across both the wet and dry seasons, a factor that minimizes the rapid atmospheric dispersal of local pollutants and allows particles to settle.
Operating within this specific climatic and geographic context, the research utilized the subtropical evergreen shrub Pittosporum tobira as the primary biological indicator. While not native to the region, this plant has been extensively cultivated throughout the municipal gardens, industrial borders, and streetscapes of Naples due to its exceptional resilience against shifting thermal dynamics and irregular precipitation. The ubiquitous presence of these uniform shrubs across all the targeted industrial, commercial, and recreational sectors provided a consistent biological matrix, allowing researchers to accurately map the spatial distribution of microfiber contamination across the entire urban gradient without introducing species-specific variables.
3.Materials and Method
The field investigation was conducted across a twelve-kilometre diameter within the municipality of Naples, Italy, where six distinct monitoring stations were established to represent a gradient of urban land uses. These sites were systematically categorized into heavy industrial zones, densely populated urban centers, and municipal green parks. At all locations, the subtropical evergreen shrub Pittosporum tobira (Thunb.) W.T.Aiton was utilized as the primary biological indicator. Native to East Asia but extensively integrated into temperate urban landscaping, this species was selected for its high environmental adaptability and known soil stabilization properties (Pan et al., 2024), making it an ideal uniform matrix for mapping spatial contamination across varying municipal sectors.
To evaluate temporal fluctuations in microfiber accumulation, sampling was executed during two contrasting meteorological periods: a dry summer campaign in July 2024, and a wet winter campaign spanning December to January. During the winter phase, collections were strategically timed to occur exactly two weeks after a major rainfall event. At each of the six sites, undamaged, fully mature leaves were harvested from the outer canopy of the shrubs at a standardized height of 100 ± 10 centimeters above the ground. Approximately ten grams of foliage, roughly equivalent to twenty individual leaves, were gathered from the circumference of each plant. From this bulk collection, researchers generated three independent biological replicates weighing one gram each by dicing and homogenizing leaf fragments of roughly 1.5 square centimeters, adapting foundational sampling protocols established by Capozzi et al. (2024).
To specifically isolate the mechanical impact of heavy precipitation on microfiber retention, a parallel experiment utilizing potted P. tobira transplants was conducted during the wet season. Six potted specimens were positioned on the roof of the Department of Biology at the University Federico II of Naples. Prior to environmental exposure, the transplants underwent a rigorous decontamination process involving a pressurized jet of running tap water followed by double-distilled water, before being dried with ambient cool air. Baseline blank samples were immediately taken to ensure the foliage was clear of pre-existing particles. For the exposure phase, three plants were left entirely unsheltered to face direct precipitation, while the remaining three were placed under a protective canopy. This setup was repeated twice with fresh transplants for exposure durations of 10 and 15 days, specifically timing the conclusion of the trials to immediately follow a heavy rain event exceeding 30 millimeters per square meter over a twenty-four-hour period (the actual recorded rainfall was 34.8 millimeters). Throughout the exposure, plants were watered biweekly, and continuous local meteorological tracking confirmed that wind speeds remained weak and comparable across both trials. Following the rain event, one-gram composite samples were collected from the transplants using the identical height and homogenization parameters applied to the native shrubs.
The recovery of anthropogenic microfibers relied on a rapid, non-destructive mechanical extraction technique. To mathematically validate this approach against seasonal shifts in leaf morphology, a preliminary test was conducted on samples from one of the urban sites using a full four-step sequential extraction outlined by Capozzi et al. (2024). This involved initial adhesive tape tearing on both the upper and lower leaf surfaces, followed by liquid extractions using water, ethanol, and wet peroxide oxidation. After confirming that the initial tape tearing consistently recovered the overwhelming majority of fibres across both seasons (77% of total fibres in summer and 68% in winter), tape tearing was adopted as the sole extraction method for the primary survey. Tape strips applied to the composite leaf samples were secured onto baking paper and systematically analysed under a Leica Wild M8 stereomicroscope equipped with an AmScope MU 100 digital camera. Microfibers were visually identified based on standardized morphological criteria (Windsor et al., 2019), with only fibres under 5 millimetres in length included in the final dataset. Stringent quality control protocols were maintained throughout the process, testing all tapes, papers, filters, and liquid reagents to ensure laboratory background contamination remained negligible, ultimately averaging just 1.73 ± 1.19 fibres per sample batch.
To physically correlate microfiber retention with seasonal anatomical shifts, fragments of summer and winter foliage were examined using Scanning Electron Microscopy coupled with Energy Dispersive Spectrometry (Jeol JSM5310). Following fixation in 2.5% glutaraldehyde, graded alcohol dehydration, and critical point drying, the gold-coated specimens were mapped to quantify microscopic surface structures. Epicuticular waxes on the winter leaves were counted across randomly selected 1-square-micrometer grids, while glandular hairs on the summer leaves were quantified using 50-square-micrometer sampling areas. All resulting biological and microfiber quantification data were processed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 27.0). An independent t-test verified the statistical effectiveness of the pre-exposure plant washing phase, while a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) assessed the main and interacting effects of land use type and seasonality, followed by Tukey’s post-hoc testing for pairwise comparisons. For non-parametric datasets, such as evaluating median microfiber lengths across seasons and comparing accumulation on sheltered versus unsheltered transplants, Kruskal-Wallis and Mann-Whitney U tests were applied, with statistical significance firmly established at a threshold of p < 0.05.
4.Result and Discussion
The operational efficacy of a single-step mechanical detachment protocol requires verification across varying seasonal states to ensure consistent analyte recovery. Methodological validation utilizing the multi-step sequential extraction at the Urban_2 site demonstrated remarkable consistency in microfiber tracking across distinct seasons. In the dry summer period, tape tearing effectively recovered the vast majority of foliar-bound particles, accounting for 77% of the total microfiber burden, while the subsequent liquid extraction steps—water, ethanol, and wet peroxide oxidation—yielded minor fractions of 14%, 5%, and 5%, respectively. During the wet winter campaign, tape tearing continued to capture the dominant portion of the microfibers at 68%, with liquid extractions shifting slightly higher to clear 16% for water, 9% for ethanol, and 8% for wet peroxide oxidation. This marginal winter decrease in primary tape efficiency suggests that ambient moisture or altered leaf surface characteristics can cause particles to bind slightly tighter to the cuticle. However, because the tape tearing step consistently captured the overriding portion of the total fibre pool across both meteorological states, the deployment of this single-step protocol is validated as a rapid, reproducible metric for large-scale spatial tracking, minimizing the need for complex, chemically intensive liquid extractions (Capozzi et al., 2024).
The biomonitoring survey executed across the specialized land-use gradient revealed that the foliar matrices of native Pittosporum tobira shrubs are highly sensitive to localized anthropogenic footprints. Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) established that the total microfiber burden was significantly controlled by land-use type (F(5, 24) = 79.00, p < 0.001). During the summer dry season, the total fibre accumulation clearly mapped the urban pollution gradient, reaching its absolute maximum at the industrial site with a count of 160 microfibers per gram of composite leaf tissue. This heavy accumulation was followed sequentially by the high-density urban nodes, with Urban_2 registering 125 fibres, Urban_1 tracking 92 fibres, and Urban_3 containing 84 fibres. The lowest deposition profile occurred within the isolated municipal parks, where Green_2 recorded 54 fibres and Green_1 tracked 48 fibres. This clear distribution pattern demonstrates that despite the potential for long-range atmospheric transport to homogenize microplastic dispersion, the continuous supply of synthetic fragments from immediate point sources—such as commercial manufacturing and vehicle tire wear—remains the primary driver of urban microfiber accumulation gradients (Dris et al., 2015; Wright et al., 2020). Proximity to localized emissions ensures a steady atmospheric fallout that easily counteracts the blending effects of macro-scale air mass movements (Jafarova et al., 2022, 2023).
The chemical and structural lifecycle of the captured pollutants is heavily influenced by their duration in the atmosphere and proximity to direct emission points. Dimensional characterization of the isolated microfibers revealed that summer leaves consistently retained significantly longer synthetic strands than their winter counterparts (p < 0.05). Specifically, the mean length of summer-captured fibres peaked across the highly active zones, tracking at 0.64 ± 1.3 mm at the industrial hub, 0.76 ± 0.58 mm at Urban_1, and 0.99 ± 1.10 mm at Urban_2. In stark contrast, winter lengths declined to 0.35 ± 0.39 mm at the industrial site and 0.43 ± 0.29 mm at Urban_2. The presence of significantly longer fibres during the summer dry months suggests a direct, uninterrupted supply of fresh structural polymers from local traffic and synthetic textiles (Amato-Lourenço et al., 2022). Conversely, the truncated length profiles observed in winter point to structural degradation driven by mechanical stress or the preferential loss of longer, higher-drag strands under the physical pressure of seasonal weathering (Qian et al., 2017).
Two-way ANOVA confirmed that seasonality exerted a dominant main effect on microfiber abundance (F(1, 24) = 241.00, p < 0.001), with total fibre counts trending substantially higher in summer than in winter across nearly all monitored zones. In the industrial sector, foliar counts dropped from a summer high of 160 to a winter baseline of 71, while Urban_1 declined from 92 to 47, Urban_2 fell from 125 to 30, and Green_2 decreased from 54 to 24. This strong seasonal pattern is primarily attributed to the intense cleaning effect of winter rainfall, which washes loosely bound particles off the cuticular surfaces (Enyoh et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2020). However, the two-way interaction between land use and season was also highly significant (F(5, 24) = 24.00, p < 0.001), identifying key anomalies where this seasonal drop did not occur. At Green_1 and Urban_3, microfiber concentrations remained statistically uniform between seasons, with Green_1 tracking 48 fibres in summer and 53 in winter, while Urban_3 shifted from 84 to 60. Field observations indicate that the sampled hedges at these specific sites were physically sheltered underneath dense overstory tree canopies. This canopy structure acts as a natural umbrella, filtering incoming atmospheric pollution while protecting the underlying P. tobira leaves from the direct wash-off and kinetic impact of open rainfall, thereby stabilizing the fibre accumulation baseline year-round (Beckett et al., 2000; McPherson et al., 2011).
The mechanical role of precipitation as an environmental clearing agent was directly confirmed through controlled exposure experiments utilizing potted P. tobira transplants. Initial baseline tracking established that a vigorous pre-exposure wash with tap water and double-distilled water significantly reduced the initial microfiber burden from a pre-cleaning mean of 20.44 ± 2.07 to a post-cleaning baseline of 9.11 ± 1.05 (p < 0.05), effectively establishing a clean slate for field deployment. Following localized ambient exposure, non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis testing identified a highly significant divergence in fibre retention between the contrasting hydrological groups. Immediately following a heavy 34.8 mm pouring rain event, the transplants left fully unsheltered exhibited a drastic loss of accumulated microfibers compared to their sheltered counterparts. This physical clearing mechanism operated uniformly across both the 10-day and 15-day trials, proving that a two-week window provides ample atmospheric exposure to establish a robust pollution signal, while confirming that direct precipitation mechanically sweeps larger, high-surface-area microplastics off the leaf surfaces (Jahandari, 2023; Zhao et al., 2015).
High-resolution Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) mapping demonstrated that seasonal variations in microfiber accumulation are heavily mediated by structural modifications in foliar micro-morphology. Summer leaves exhibited highly specialized anatomical traits on their upper surfaces, characterized by irregular cuticular ridges and a dense concentration of active glandular trichomes, tracking at an average density of 20.6 ± 2.2 hairs per 50 μm² with lengths spanning 1 to 5 μm. Conversely, the lower summer surface featured distinctly sunken stomata alongside minimal, highly degraded residual epicuticular waxes. These summer glandular hairs and specialized ridges provide optimal physical anchor points that trap and hold incoming airborne microfibers. This mechanical retention is likely enhanced by sticky glandular exudates that promote particle adhesion during prolonged dry spells.
In contrast, winter foliage completely lacked these glandular trichomes. Instead, the winter cuticular landscape was heavily dominated by dense crystalline epicuticular waxes. These structures appeared as elongated rods on the upper leaf surface, yielding a dense concentration of 16 ± 2 μm⁻² in highly covered zones and dropping to 2.5 ± 1.8 μm⁻² in low-coverage areas, while transforming into tiny, microscopic scales on the lower surface. These rod-shaped structures and microscopic scales create a more uniform, crystalline surface texture that reduces the overall contact area for larger, fibrous particles. Combined with the absence of adhesive trichome hairs, this winter wax structure significantly lowers the leaf's particle adhesion capacity, allowing winter rains to easily wash the microplastics away. This clear structural shift underscores that comparative microfiber biomonitoring surveys must be tightly indexed to seasonal states and localized rainfall boundaries to avoid substantial underestimation of airborne polymer concentrations (Prigioniero et al., 2023).
5.Conclusion
The strategic evaluation of Pittosporum tobira leaves as biological indicators validates this species as a reliable and highly sensitive tool for mapping airborne anthropogenic microfiber pollution across complex urban gradients. By utilizing a simplified, one-step mechanical detachment technique consisting solely of cuticular tape tearing, the extraction framework successfully differentiates shifting contamination intensities across contrasting industrial, residential, and recreational land uses. The empirical evidence confirms that proximity to direct emissions heavily regulates foliar pollutant loading, allowing the biological matrix to generate an accurate spatial map of synthetic microfiber fallout despite the mixing influences of macro-scale atmospheric transport (Capozzi et al., 2024).
The investigation establishes that meteorological factors and shifting foliar traits exert a dominant, interactive control over microfiber bioaccumulation dynamics. Total synthetic fibre retention peaks heavily during the dry summer months, driven by prolonged periods of low precipitation and the specialized development of adhesive glandular trichomes on the leaf cuticle. Conversely, winter accumulation experiences a substantial decline due to the physical wash-off effect of seasonal rainfall and an anatomical transition toward crystalline epicuticular waxes that reduce particle adhesion. The introduction of P. tobira transplants directly confirms this hydrological mechanism, demonstrating that open exposure to heavy pouring rain significantly clears accumulated polymers, particularly the longest, high-drag synthetic fragments.
Ultimately, these findings prove that while P. tobira functions as an excellent natural sponge for atmospheric microfibers, seasonal and meteorological parameters must be tightly synchronized across sampling networks to prevent severe underestimations of airborne plastic concentrations. Monitoring sensitivity is optimized under low-rainfall, summer conditions, indicating that comparative regional surveys should be strictly indexed to identical seasonal windows. Because the proposed tape-tearing extraction protocol is rapid, cost-effective, and entirely green, it provides a highly deployable method for large-scale air quality networks. This foundational framework offers an accessible methodology for citizen science initiatives, providing a practical tool to elevate public awareness of atmospheric plastic waste and track synthetic polymer dispersion within urban ecosystems.

Responses of tree defoliators to traffic-derived particulate matter and trace elements along a roadside pollution gradient Hanna Moniuszko1, Robert Popek, Arkadiusz Przybysz & Adrian Łukowski
1.Background
Airborne particulate matter and traffic-derived trace elements represent persistent, escalating hazards within urban and peri-urban ecosystems. To mitigate these atmospheric pollutants, municipal planning frequently relies on green infrastructure and roadside vegetation to facilitate phytoremediation. The leaves and stems of adjacent trees and shrubs function as passive biological filters, intercepting suspended particles and trapping complex mixtures of inorganic metals and polyaromatic hydrocarbons. Additionally, toxins settling onto the soil surface frequently migrate into the root zone, ultimately accumulating within the vegetative organs. While the sequestration of these compounds serves a vital air-purifying function, it fundamentally alters the chemical landscape of the foliage, transitioning the plant from a simple biological filter into a highly concentrated repository for environmental toxins.
The ecological consequences of this phytoremediation extend far beyond the immediate plant-pollutant dynamic, imposing severe, hidden costs on local trophic networks. Arthropods inhabiting the phyllosphere are forced into direct, continuous contact with these sequestered materials. Phytophagous insects, in particular, inevitably ingest particulate matter bound to cuticular waxes or embedded within leaf tissues during routine foraging. This mechanical and chemical exposure has been shown to suppress biomass and disrupt population stability. Despite these documented pressures, a prevailing assumption within contemporary literature suggests that many chewing herbivores—specifically recognized pest species—possess a generalized tolerance to heavily degraded environments. However, categorizing these insects as uniformly resilient risks oversimplifying the complex behavioural and demographic penalties exacted by chronic roadside pollution.
This assumption of tolerance is particularly questionable for species characterized by narrow host ranges and low spatial mobility. Specialized arthropods lack the ecological flexibility to abandon contaminated foraging grounds, making them exceptionally vulnerable when their required host plants are situated along high-traffic corridors. To rigorously examine the limits of this supposed pollution tolerance, the orchard ermine moth, Yponomeuta padella (Lepidoptera: Yponomeutidae), serves as an optimal ecological model. Operating as an oligophagous defoliator, this moth relies exclusively on specific woody hosts such as Crataegus (hawthorn) and Prunus (plum), both of which are ubiquitous fixtures in urban roadside landscaping. Because the female strictly dictates the oviposition site and the resulting larvae construct restrictive, tent-like silk structures, the caterpillars are effectively imprisoned within a rigid developmental window. This spatial confinement forces them to endure the localized chemical realities of their immediate hatching site, removing their ability to migrate toward higher-quality food resources.
The present study addresses the critical knowledge gap surrounding herbivore vulnerability by evaluating the developmental and behavioural responses of Y. padella exposed to authentic, traffic-derived particulate gradients. Utilizing Crataegus monogyna and Prunus cerasifera harvested from distinct proximity zones along a heavily trafficked roadway, the investigation aims to deconstruct the mechanisms of pollution tolerance. The primary objectives are to determine whether larvae exhibit active foraging avoidance when presented with varying levels of foliar contamination, and to quantify the severe physiological costs—measured through survival trajectories, adult emergence dynamics, and final body mass—resulting from obligatory feeding on polluted tissues. The core hypothesis posits that larvae will actively reject contaminated foliage if provided a choice; however, because natural field conditions physically restrict them to the polluted tissues selected during oviposition, this inescapable exposure will critically impair their physiological development and adult fitness.
2.Study Area
The field investigation was carried out in the northern sector of Poznań, a major city situated in western Poland. The specific experimental plot was positioned near Lechicka Street (coordinates 52.4333665°N, 16.9584921°E), an area that seamlessly integrates a municipal park with a heavily trafficked urban expressway known as National Road number 92. This exact location was deliberately chosen because the roadway experiences a massive volume of continuous vehicular circulation, notably including a high proportion of heavy-duty freight trucks. This intense and unrelenting traffic profile creates a reliable, severe emission source necessary for evaluating the physiological and behavioural responses of insects to traffic-derived pollutants. Furthermore, the structural layout of the surrounding green infrastructure provided an ideal spatial framework to establish a clear, real-world contamination gradient while keeping overarching ecological variables—such as vertical canopy structure, light availability, and background vegetation composition—strictly uniform across the entire testing perimeter.
To accurately map the decline of particulate matter and trace elements moving away from the active emission source, the spatial sampling design was divided into three distinct environmental tiers. The most severe contamination zone, designated as the roadside variant, consisted of trees situated just one to two meters from the asphalt edge, with their canopies directly facing the oncoming traffic. This immediate proximity guaranteed maximum exposure to raw exhaust fumes, resuspended road dust, and severe acoustic stress. Moving slightly inward, the secondary sidewalk variant encompassed shrubs and trees that were partially buffered by an initial layer of vegetation. Although still positioned within the active roadside strip, this intermediate tier experienced a slightly mitigated influx of direct automotive emissions. Finally, the baseline control zone was established behind a massive, five-meter-tall acoustic wall that physically isolated the neighbouring urban park from the highway. Trees in this control sector grew along a sheltered forest edge, benefiting from a highly stable microclimate and a drastic reduction in traffic-associated ecological stressors.
Within this stratified landscape, the investigation focused exclusively on the common hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna) and the cherry plum (Prunus cerasifera). Both woody species are ubiquitous components of urban and roadside landscaping, heavily valued for their capacity to tolerate the intense abiotic stress characteristic of modern communication routes. Operating within these publicly accessible spaces, the researchers conducted non-destructive, manual foliage collections during the peak of full leaf expansion in late May. To ensure the collected leaves retained their maximum accumulated pollutant load without interference from meteorological wash-off, the sampling was meticulously timed during a dry weather window. Throughout that month, total precipitation reached only about 25 millimetres, and the collection specifically followed a ten-day period with absolutely zero recorded rainfall. This rigorous site selection and precise temporal coordination ensured that the subsequent biological assays accurately reflected the stark chemical realities of inhabiting a highly polluted, traffic-adjacent ecosystem.
3.Materials and Method
The sampling campaign targeted the evergreen shrub Pittosporum tobira across a twelve-kilometre gradient within the municipality of Naples, capturing six distinct sites representing industrial, urban, and green park land uses. To capture seasonal deposition dynamics, collections were split into two distinct meteorological windows: a dry summer phase in July 2024 and a wet winter phase spanning December to January. The winter collection was carefully scheduled to occur precisely two weeks following a major precipitation event. At every monitoring station, undamaged, fully expanded leaves were harvested evenly around the outer canopy of the shrubs at a standardized elevation of 100 ± 10 cm above the ground. Approximately 10 g of foliage (roughly twenty leaves) was gathered per plant. From this bulk collection, researchers excised leaf fragments of approximately 1.5 cm² and homogenized them to create three independent 1 g biological replicates per site, adapting the foundational sampling protocols developed by Capozzi et al. (2024).
To isolate the mechanical influence of heavy rainfall on microfiber retention, a parallel experiment was conducted during the wet season using potted P. tobira transplants. Six plants were stationed on the roof of the Department of Biology at the University of Naples Federico II. Before deployment, the foliage underwent rigorous decontamination using a pressurized wash of running tap water followed by a double-distilled water rinse, and was subsequently dried with cool air. Baseline blank samples confirmed the successful removal of pre-existing microfibers. During the exposure phase, three transplants were left entirely unsheltered to endure direct rainfall, while the remaining three were placed beneath a protective canopy. This array was deployed twice, testing exposure durations of 10 and 15 days. Both trials were specifically terminated immediately after a severe rain event exceeding 30 mm m⁻² in 24 hours (recorded actual rainfall hit 34.8 mm). Transplants received biweekly watering, and local meteorological data verified that ambient wind velocities remained consistently low across both trial periods. Post-exposure sampling mirrored the native plant protocol to generate 1 g composite analytical samples.
The recovery of anthropogenic microfibers relied on a rapid, non-destructive mechanical detachment technique. To validate the stability of this approach against seasonal shifts in leaf morphology, a preliminary multi-step sequential extraction (Capozzi et al., 2024) was tested on samples from the Urban_2 site. This calibration involved initial adhesive tape tearing followed by subsequent liquid extractions utilizing water, ethanol, and wet peroxide oxidation. Because the initial tape tearing consistently recovered the vast majority of particles across both seasons (77% of the total load in summer and 68% in winter), it was adopted as the sole, streamlined extraction method for the primary survey. Tape strips applied to the composite leaf samples were secured onto baking paper and systematically analyzed under a Leica Wild M8 stereomicroscope equipped with an AmScope MU 100 digital camera. Microfibers were identified using the standardized morphological criteria defined by Windsor et al. (2019). Only fibers measuring under 5 mm were included in the final dataset.
To correlate microfiber retention with seasonal anatomical shifts, leaf fragments measuring 2–3 mm were examined using a Jeol JSM5310 Scanning Electron Microscope equipped with Energy Dispersive Spectrometry. Following fixation in 2.5% glutaraldehyde, the tissues were dehydrated through a graded alcohol series, subjected to critical point drying, and gold-coated on aluminum stubs. Epicuticular wax structures on the winter leaves were quantified across twenty random 1 μm² grids (split equally between high-density and low-density areas), while glandular hairs on the summer leaves were mapped using 50 μm² sampling zones. Throughout all laboratory procedures, stringent quality control measures were enforced. Tapes, oven papers, glass-fiber filters, and all liquid reagents were routinely examined to ensure that background contamination remained negligible, ultimately averaging just 1.73 ± 1.19 stray fibers across all control materials.
All biometric and microfiber quantification data were processed utilizing IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 27.0) and Microsoft Excel. Data normality and variance homogeneity were verified using Shapiro-Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. An independent t-test confirmed the statistical efficacy of the pre-exposure plant washing phase. To evaluate the primary survey data, a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) assessed the main and interactive effects of land-use type and seasonality on microfiber accumulation, followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post-hoc test. For datasets failing parametric assumptions—such as the comparison of median microfiber lengths and the evaluation of sheltered versus unsheltered transplants—non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis and Mann-Whitney U tests were employed. The threshold for statistical significance was uniformly established at p < 0.05.
4.Result and Discussion
Initial evaluations of the host plants revealed fundamental, species-driven differences in leaf physical architecture. Prunus cerasifera naturally developed a higher specific leaf area (190.01 cm2/g) and greater structural toughness (156.99 gf/mm2) compared to the denser, softer leaves of Crataegus monogyna (169.47 cm2/g and 146.96 gf/mm2). These structural baseline differences were strictly governed by plant genetics (p < 0.0001). The actual growing environment—whether adjacent to heavy traffic or sequestered behind an acoustic barrier—had no statistically significant impact on the physical toughness or specific area of the leaves.
However, the localized environment heavily dictated the chemical and particulate load those leaves carried. Particulate matter deposition reliably followed a steep, predictable spatial gradient: isolated control areas maintained the cleanest foliage, intermediate sidewalk zones carried a moderate load, and the direct roadside canopy was severely contaminated. This spatial pattern held true across virtually all PM size fractions. P. cerasifera proved to be a more efficient passive filter, accumulating higher total PM loads than the hawthorn, likely due to canopy or micro-structural variations.
Trace element (TE) analysis confirmed that the immediate roadside acts as a sink for vehicle-associated metals. Copper, iron, and strontium peaked sharply on the roadside leaves and gradually dissipated moving toward the control site, directly reflecting their origins in brake wear and vehicular abrasion. Highly toxic elements like arsenic, cadmium, and lead were detected exclusively in the heavily trafficked roadside and sidewalk zones, completely vanishing from the isolated control samples. Other detected elements (barium, zinc, molybdenum) showed scattered, non-uniform distributions, which likely ties back to localized soil geochemistries and regular plant micronutrient uptake rather than direct exhaust exposure.
A prevailing assumption in urban ecology suggests that defoliating pest species are largely immune to degraded habitats. The choice assays actively dismantle this idea. When late-instar Yponomeuta padella caterpillars were offered a three-way choice between foliage from the distinct pollution zones, they overwhelmingly rejected the contaminated food. Larvae actively sought out and selected the clean control leaves 66% of the time for P. cerasifera and 68% of the time for C. monogyna.
Conversely, the heavily polluted roadside leaves were selected in only 14% of the trials for both host species (p < 0.001). The caterpillars demonstrated a sharp, active capacity to detect and avoid traffic-derived chemical burdens when granted the freedom to forage. This contrasts with field observations of other species, such as the western tussock moth, which has been shown to preferentially select highway-adjacent foliage in certain contexts. The intense aversion shown by Y. padella proves that pollution tolerance is highly species-specific and not a universal trait among chewing defoliators.
While caterpillars can easily avoid polluted leaves in a laboratory setting, real-world field constraints are entirely unforgiving. Y. padella larvae are relatively immobile, trapped within communal silken tents spun by their mothers during oviposition. Because they are forced to consume whatever foliage surrounds their immediate hatching site, the physiological costs of roadside living become severe. Tracking emergence dynamics via logistic modeling revealed that caterpillars forced to consume a roadside diet suffered stunted growth rates and significantly delayed inflection points compared to those eating sidewalk or control leaves.
The ultimate biological penalty manifested in pupal eclosion success. Moths reared on clean control foliage successfully emerged at a rate of 90.14%. This demographic success dropped to 82.50% on sidewalk diets, and plummeted to just 77.13% on the roadside variants. This survival decline was heavily driven by the collection site itself (p < 0.0001), with the specific host plant species offering no significant buffering effect against the pollution. Furthermore, the physical quality of the survivors deteriorated drastically. Both male and female moths achieved their absolute lowest final body masses when restricted to the roadside diet (p < 0.001).
These physiological and demographic declines mirror laboratory toxicological findings seen in non-pest species, such as stunted pupal mass in butterflies exposed to artificial haze or direct genotoxic DNA damage observed in larvae feeding on PM-laced vegetation. The heavy particulate and trace element loading acts as a dual-action stressor: it directly introduces heavy metals and toxic compounds into the insect gut while simultaneously degrading the host plant's underlying nutritional baseline. Ultimately, road verges operate as a severe ecological filter. While they successfully trap airborne toxins—providing a localized environmental phytoremediation service—they simultaneously create highly toxic microhabitats that actively suppress the survival, growth, and reproductive fitness of the insect populations trapped within them.
5.Conclusion
The scientific investigation into the responses of Yponomeuta padella to roadside pollution underscores a fundamental tension in urban ecology: the ecosystem services provided by plants—such as the filtration of airborne particulate matter—often come at a significant, hidden cost to the phyllosphere-associated arthropods that inhabit them. By utilizing a real-world pollution gradient to examine the physical and chemical impacts of traffic-derived stressors, this study challenges the prevailing, and perhaps overly optimistic, assumption that insect pest species are inherently tolerant of degraded urban environments.
The data indicates that the roadside environment acts as a multifaceted ecological filter. While the host plants, *Crataegus monogyna* and *Prunus cerasifera*, effectively sequester traffic-derived pollutants, they simultaneously function as toxic substrates for the larvae of  Y. padella. The study reveals that the physical presence of particulate matter (PM) and the chemical concentration of trace elements (TEs) on leaf surfaces are not merely passive environmental markers; they represent active stressors that restructure trophic interactions. The clear spatial pattern—where total PM, copper, iron, and strontium concentrations increase significantly from control zones to roadside corridors—links the physical geography of transport infrastructure directly to the physiological performance of the insects.
The behavioural responses of the larvae in choice assays provide some of the most compelling evidence regarding the non-uniform impacts of pollution. The strong avoidance of contaminated foliage suggests that these specialized folivores possess sensory mechanisms to detect and shun low-quality, high-toxicity resources. However, the demographic data highlights the disconnect between behavioural preference and environmental reality. In natural conditions, the moth's rigid oviposition strategy and the larvae’s limited mobility lock them into a specific developmental trajectory. When larvae are forced to feed on polluted tissues, the cumulative impact of chemical ingestion and surface contact results in demonstrably reduced survival rates, delayed emergence, and lower adult biomass.
Ultimately, this study contributes to a more nuanced understanding of urban ecological health by emphasizing that the impact of pollution is context-dependent. The survival success of Y. padella, which declined significantly from 90.1% in control settings to 77.1% at the roadside, reflects the intense pressure that roadside habitats exert on insect fitness. This suggests that the "tolerance" often attributed to pest species may mask sub-lethal fitness costs that could fundamentally alter population dynamics and habitat suitability in the long term. Consequently, these findings argue for a critical reassessment of urban greening strategies, suggesting that the ecological degradation of roadside verges poses a serious risk to insect diversity and may, in time, compromise the sustainability of urban ecosystems that depend on these intricate plant–insect relationships.
